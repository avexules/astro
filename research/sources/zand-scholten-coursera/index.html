My thoughts on scientific knowledge

(margin note, top right) different sources that show transparent references based on real observation → EVIDENCE

Non-scientific: (subjective, error prone)
Intuition / belief → doesn’t mean true; there can be opposing beliefs. We can count consensus and believe the majority → also not always true
Consensus
Authority → also not objective

Opinion // Evidence

  • ↳ Problem: selective observations, informal logic

Scientific: Systematic observation. Formal Logic, consistently applied.

  1. empirically testable (observation, real, physical)
  2. replicable (repeated confirmation, keeps being true)
  3. objective (no matter who performs the study; clear assumptions, concepts, procedures)
  4. transparent (publicly shared)
  5. falsifiable (we can imagine what a contradiction would be)
  6. logically consistent / coherent (hypothesis and conclusion…)

Scientific attitude → critical, open, transparent. Then science works like an evolutionary system where only the fittest, i.e. most plausible, hypothesis persist.

Left page

Empirical Cycle — hypothetico-deductive approach

Testing → Evaluation → Observation … research question → Deduction ← Induction → prediction / methodology ← hypothesis (cyclical diagram: Testing leads to Evaluation, which leads to Observation/research question, which leads down to Deduction and Induction, which lead to prediction/methodology and hypothesis respectively)

Confirmation / Disconfirmation

  • a confirmation is never conclusive
  • hypotheses cannot be proven but can be provisionally supported…
  • plausible failures → research design, background variables, methodological issues
  • often hypotheses are preserved and redesign research, or adjusted by specification

Criteria for evaluation

  • Reliability → Replicability
  • Validity → reflecting reality
    • ↳ construct: measured and manipulated as intended (accurately)
    • internal: causal relation, maybe plausible alt. explanation
    • external: results may generalize to other patterns; the findings are the same over settings

Right page

Causality

  1. Cause → Effect are connected
  2. Cause precedes Effect
  3. Cause and Effect covary consistently
  4. No alternative explanations (C → E, Alt. ✗) — Difficult!

Correlation does not imply Causation

Threats to INTERNAL validity → due to participants

  • Maturation: natural change over time of research — solution: control group
  • Selection: systematic differences in the exp. group — solution: randomization
  • Selection by maturation: systematic differences in maturation — solution: also randomization

Threats to INTERNAL validity → due to instruments

  • Low construct validity: no focus on the intended concepts, so no conclusions can be made on the intended hypothesis
  • Instrumentation: change in measurement systems over the course of the research
  • Testing / sensitization: when the measurements/experiments affect behavior
    • ↳ control group! but may not be enough

Page 5 (notebook pp. 16–17[?])

Left page

Threats to INTERNAL validity (still) → due to artificiality

  • Experimenter expectancy: solution: experimenter blind design
  • Demand characteristics: participants change behaviour when they know which group they are in… — solution: double blind design and COVER STORY → minimize bias

Threats to INTERNAL validity → due to research setup

  • Ambiguous temporal precedence: when hypothesised cause is unclear to precede the observed effect.
  • History: some event that provides an alt. effect, e.g. “the experimenter faints in the experiment” — hard to eliminate, you need to discard some results/observations
  • Mortality: drop-outs of the study, especially when exp. and control group drop-outs differ strongly — solution: document reasons for drop-out

Right page

RESEARCH VARIABLES

Construct → abstract phenomena Variable vs. constant: variable is an operationalized, concrete expression of a construct Independent vs. dependent variable

  • ↳ when there’s cause and effect in the hypothesis
    • cause / explanatory / input / predictor → VARIABLE
    • effect / response / outcome / output → VARIABLE

There are other extraneous properties, variables of interest, that may be cause for alt. explanations:

  • Confounders (also called “lurking variables”): related to both independent and dependent variables, and not included in the hypothesis as well as often not measured
  • Control variables: like a confounder but is part of the study and measured
  • Background variables: not directly in relation with the independent and dependent variable, but the generalizability, like sex, age, ethnic/cultural, socio-economic status, education level… — you need to control for it or keep it constant

Page 6 (notebook pp. 18–19[?])

Left page

RESEARCH DESIGNS

True Experiments (maximize internal validity, e.g. by randomized control trials (RCT))

  • Manipulation: eliminating ambiguous temporal precedence by having the cause under full control so it definitely precedes the effect — creating a situation where the causal variable is present
  • Comparison: compare to an effect where the cause is absent (eliminates Maturation)
  • Random assignment: randomization for assigning to experimental & control (no systematic difference, in theory, other than the causal variable)
  • and Replication — to rule out any chance

Experiments with different levels of the independent variable

  • ↳ separate and combined effect ⇒ factorial design
  • ↳ exposition of participants to all levels of the independent variable ⇒ repeated measures design

Right page

Factorial Design

Several independent variables (or ‘factors’) — ↳ simultaneous investigation

e.g. 3 different dosages of a drug × 2 different genders = 6 different conditions

⇒ effects of dosage, effects of genders } main effect interaction effect { effects of dosage and gender combined (two way) }

There may be “incomplete designs” where not every factorial condition is observed.

Repeated Measures Design & Longitudinal design (longer)

Between vs Within subject design

  • between: participants are split amongst conditions
  • within: all participants experience all conditions

within & between can also be combined → more efficient → correlational or quasi-experimental

Randomization with repeated measures in within-subject

  • ↳ randomization is necessary, else risk of Maturation or Habituation effect
  • ⇒ order of conditions randomized for COUNTERBALANCING, making sure with blocking that no order is over-/under-represented!

Standard Research Designs — EXPERIMENTAL

  • Two-Group: N → randomization → Control (cause absent) / Experiment (cause present)
  • Two-Group Pre/Post (for small groups): N → random. → PRE (on the dependent) → Control → POST (also on the dependent) / PRE (on the dependent) → Experimental → POST — good when threat of maturation, or to quantify increase/decrease. Pretest can sometimes sensitize, have practice effect, or alert to purpose.
  • Solomon 4-Group: N → random. → [PRE random. → Control → POST; PRE random. → Experiment → POST; random. → Control → POST; random. → Experiment → POST]
  • Within (Repeated Measures): N → rand. → A → Control → TEST → Experiment → POST; B → Experiment → TEST → Control → POST — randomization for A/B determines the order of the conditions experienced.

Left page

When random assignment is impossible, the study is quasi-experimental — a method to approximate randomisation is called matching.

MATCHING: when random assignment is not possible because of pragmatic/ethical reasons, or the independent variable is an individual difference variable

  • ↳ on relevant background variables to try and exclude alt. explanation

Phenomenon of Undermatching: due to regression to[wards] the mean (when a second measurement scores lower/more average than a first measurement) — can occur if related to variables of interest & measured with some errors (as per usual in social sciences)

QUASI-EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN

  • when it lacks Randomization. Sometimes even Manipulation or Comparison — BUT!
  • investigates causal relation
  • values are selected, not manipulated (like Correlational studies) (of independent variable)

Standard Designs:

  1. Static group comparison (like Two-Group)
  2. Pre/posttest non-equivalent control group — ↳ controls for threat of selection & maturation (pre) (post)
  3. Interrupted time-series (one group pre/posttest) — ↳ history threats!
  4. Replicated interrupted time-series → with a control group, to rule out history threat

Right page

CORRELATIONAL DESIGNS

  • no manipulation of independent variable — OR —
  • no independent variable identified, because the hypothesis doesn’t specify a causal relationship

⇒ no manipulation, no selection, just measuring!

Standard Designs:

  1. Cross-sectional — large group of N, several properties measured at once
  2. Time-series — e.g. 1 person is measured often, in fixed intervals
  3. Panel design (time-series cross-sectional, TSCS) — so combo of 1 & 2. ↳ Cohort, when the cross-section shares a certain property repeated

OTHER IMPORTANT TERMS

  • Longitudinal → general term for measuring repeatedly over long period of time, no matter if EXPERIMENTAL, QUASI-EXPERIMENTAL or CORRELATIONAL DESIGNS.
  • Time-Series either → CORRELATIONAL, when it’s about 1 person, or → QUASI-EXPERIMENTAL, when it’s groups; sometimes → EXPERIMENTAL, 1 person measured over time in all conditions (N=1 study)
  • Repeated Measures → EXPERIMENTAL, at least 1 independent variable was manipulated or selected

SPECIAL / OTHER DESIGNS

  • Case Study — one person or a group as a whole. Quantitative: time-series / N=1 study. Qualitative: generating hypotheses more than testing. (Interesting: case studies can be of good use to reject a hypothesis ⇒ negative case analysis)
  • Evaluation research — evaluate effect of policy/program (in sociology, communication, political sciences); can be summative: check outcome, can be formative: check process. Applied, usually non-experimental because of ethical reasons.
  • Intervention study (developmental, clinical psychology) — effect of treatment on individuals, usually (quasi-)experimental.
  • Validation study (psychometrics, sociometrics) — assess quality of a measurement instrument like a survey or questionnaire/test. Check if answers show high internal consistencies. Check if expected patterns emerge.

Page 13 (notebook p. 34)

For probability sampling you need a Sampling Frame.

Accuracy of Sample estimate — “margin of error” → estimate of how much the sample average will differ from the population average.

Confidence Interval ↳ provide a fairly confident statement that a certain percentage of the population will lie within a certain range from the sample estimate.

Two basic types of probability sampling:

Simple Random Sampling Systematic Sampling
independent; every unique combination is equally likely first element is randomly chosen, every next element is systematically chosen based on the previous one
Stratified random sampling Multi-Stage Cluster Sampling
stratum (like gender) ↳ then random (same proportion of any) sub-population; weighing subsamples according to stratum size Sampling frame (like school districts); selection stratified; further random selection

↓ larger margin of error & harder to obtain, but dramatical reduction in cost & effort

SAMPLING ERRORS — difference from population to sample from randomness

True value (of population) is always unknown, so we cannot provide an exact ERROR of the sample. BUT we can estimate sampling error:

  • ↳ draw several random samples from population
  • ↳ standard error → margin of error → confidence interval

Confidence interval allows us to say that with repeated sampling, in a certain percentage of these samples, the true population value will differ from the sample value by no more than the margin of error.

Research Integrity

  • FRAUD → Fabrication or Falsification of Data
  • PLAGIARISM → redundant information and copyright infringement
  • CONFLICTS OF INTEREST → frequent in medical sciences, when companies hold an interest in the outcome of the study
  • PERSONAL VALUES → not accepting critique and counter-arguments

Questionable Research Practices — Massaging of Findings

  • HARKing: Hypothesizing After Results are Known (Posteriori)
  • P-Hacking: manipulate data e.g. p-value (probability) more favorable based on arbitrary choices
  • Cherry Picking: omission of results favorable to hypothesis
  • Data Snooping: collection of data until results show a favorable p-value — ↳ sample size should be determined beforehand

⇒ most important is transparency to the READER, so they can evaluate for themselves if this is critical.
