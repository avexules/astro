How to keep them apart.

  • Deduction applies a rule: rule + case → result.
  • Induction builds a rule: case + result → rule.
  • Abduction guesses a cause: rule + result → case. It runs the rule backwards.

Why abduction is not valid deduction. Its schema (H → E, E, therefore H) is the fallacy of affirming the consequent: E can be true while H is false. Abduction is therefore a way of proposing hypotheses, not of proving them. What turns it into a method is comparing rival explanations of the same E and keeping the best one, which is why it is also called inference to the best explanation. Later the hypothesis is tested by deduction (derive further predictions from H, then check them), and the observations that follow feed back into induction and new abductions. In short: abduction proposes H, deduction derives what H predicts, induction learns from what is observed.

Heliocentric model proposed by Aristarchus, picked up by Kopernikus, Galileo and proved by Newton…

Comparing two Geocentric models: eccentric and epicyclic, you can see both are geometrically and empirically equivalent. Meaning both can explain looping behaviours of planets that simpler geocentric models could not. But with such competing models, we cannot decide which one of both is true because both models are empirically equivalent. The two models predict the same observations. ⇢ Underdetermination Disagreement on the theoretical interpretation, even though you can agree on the empirical observation. So what can be done?

  • Create and test a new model altogether
  • Try a new understanding of reality

Underdetermination:

  • empirically equivalent theories
  • problem with theory-choice
  • empirical adequacy vs. non-empirical virtues (simplicity? …)
  • cf. statistical model selection

Difference between empirical equivalence and empirical adequacy:

  • Empirical equivalence compares two theories with each other. T1 and T2 are empirically equivalent if they make the same predictions about everything observable, now and in the future, so no possible observation could tell them apart.
  • Empirical adequacy compares one theory with the world. T is empirically adequate if what it says about observable things and events is true: it fits all the observations, past, present and future. Van Fraassen (1980) takes this, not truth, as the aim of science.
Empirical equivalence Empirical adequacy
Relation between theory and theory theory and observations
Question do T1 and T2 predict the same observations? does T fit the observations?
Can be tested by data? no, by definition data cannot separate T1 from T2 yes, an observation that contradicts T makes it inadequate
Consequence underdetermination: data cannot decide between T1 and T2 the theory stays or is replaced when an observation contradicts it

Examples:

  • Equivalence, from the lecture: the eccentric and the epicyclic geocentric models. Both reproduce the looping paths of the planets, so the observations cannot decide which one is true.
  • Equivalence: Bohmian mechanics and standard quantum mechanics make the same predictions for measurement outcomes, so experiments cannot separate them. Lorentz’s ether theory and Einstein’s special relativity were empirically equivalent in their predictions too, and physicists chose relativity for non-empirical reasons such as simplicity.
  • Adequacy: Newton’s gravitation fitted the observed planetary motion for 200 years. It then met two problems. The orbit of Uranus did not fit; adding a further planet (Neptune, predicted by Le Verrier in 1846 and then observed) restored the fit. The 43 arcseconds per century of extra Mercury perihelion shift could not be fixed that way, so Newton’s theory was empirically inadequate there, and general relativity fits it.
  • Adequacy: the Ptolemaic system was adequate for the naked-eye observations of its time but not for Galileo’s telescope observations, such as the phases of Venus.

If T1 and T2 are empirically equivalent, they are both adequate or both inadequate, because the observations treat them the same. So adequacy can never choose between equivalent theories. That is why underdetermination needs something beyond data: non-empirical virtues such as simplicity, or in statistics, model-selection criteria. The reverse does not hold. Two theories may both be adequate for the data so far without being equivalent, because they may still disagree about future observations. That is the curve-fitting example above: a complex polynomial fits all collected points (adequate so far) but may predict the next points badly, where a simpler function might not.

Popper (Austrian philosopher) * Start of 20th century. In a time of revolutions in science, just after WW1. Trained as a psychologist. Psychoanalysis was something quite different to e.g. Physics. He supports a concept called FALSIFIABILITY, meaning that if you want to confirm a theory, you should not be able to falsify it/reject it through opposite evidence. (Because of theory-ladeness, confirming a theory with a bit of evidence is not that hard…) So confirmation of the theory may come after you fail to falsify your theory.

H → E … but if you find ¬E → ¬H is true

Eliminating Possibilities: What does my theory not accommodate? I want to research what may not be supported by my theory. If I do the opposite, I am prone to confirmation bias. There are a couple of theories that inherently cannot be falsified, like “Rational Choice Theory”… what about them?

Summary For Popper, you need virtually infinite pieces of evidence for verification; hence it is much more efficient to go by falsifiability. Falsifiability as a criterion of demarcation between science and non-science. E.g.:

Kuhn: Paradigms & Revolutions

Main Work (1962)
While Popper suggested always being in a state of doubt, hence the Falsifiability approach, Thomas Kuhn (physicist) sees two main problems with Poppers view:

  • not much choice & falsification in scientific history
  • there is probably no real individual approach to science; significance & statistical error etc. are inherent to the scientific community (know-how of theory and evidence)

Main difference is the perspective: Popper → how science should be done; Kuhn → how science is done (with respect to institutions, norms, influence …)

Main difference in Normality: Popper → there are always new Hypotheses; Kuhn → there is one paradigm at a time with no alternative

PARADIGMS:
Kuhn explains that science works in Paradigms. A Paradigm holds the hypotheses, the models, the tools, the values, the exemplars, the know-how, all with implicit/tacit knowledge (e.g. what you get teached at university, what your supervisors tell you, etc.). Paradigms are hard to change. The Paradigm defines the way evidence is collected (Kuhn attributes a lot to → Theory-ladeness).

Conjunctions of Hypotheses make it so that Falsification shows at least one of the Hypotheses is false. Kuhn states that when you look at Physics History, there are not so many falsifications; e.g. there were 3 centuries of non-contradicted Newton Theories. He claims that the evidence and hypotheses are part of the Theory ⇢ PARADIGMS → mutual agreements of science community

  • The whole Paradigm touches the evidence
  • Community is big for Kuhn
  • If evidence shows error in a Paradigm, this may not fall back on the scientist as much as on the paradigm
T__O__← the Paradigm
E_____
R_____

“the Theory straps itself” → Theory-ladeness is very strong for Kuhn.

Kuhn claims that Humanities, Social Sciences, Economics etc. don’t have a Paradigm (yet).

3 STAGES OF DEVELOPMENT OF A SCIENCE

  1. Premature Science:
    • Schools period
    • No Paradigm yet
    • No specific shared practice; missing agreements
      ↓ vague process, some unification happening
  2. Mature Science:
    • You have a Paradigm that you can agree on (the only way for a Science to mature)
    • Once you reach the Paradigm, you will not come back to Premature stage, you may only evolve the Paradigm or revolutionise it
  3. Extraordinary Science:
    • Revolution of a Paradigm to a new one (this is a complete change of how Science is done → not to be confounded with inflational use of the term ‘revolution’)
    • Old Paradigm enters a crisis, so a new paradigm is built and suggested

What happens when a Paradigm needs to change?

  • You see anomalies you cannot explain through the Paradigm
  • People come up with an opposite series of Hypotheses

INCOMMENSURABILITY

  • Semantically
    • Different Paradigms assign a different meaning to the same concept
  • Methodological
    • Differences in standards, aims, methods and values
      ↳ sufficient conditions, but it is disputed if there is necessity for both types of Incommensurability
  • Old Paradigm may be very successful, a lot of models and concepts are covered → you may need a certain optimism to stand in for a new Paradigm
