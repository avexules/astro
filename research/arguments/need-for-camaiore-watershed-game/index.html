Summary

Camaiore needs a bespoke game because the existing watershed games split the job in two and none fits the place. Controlled experiments on upstream-downstream cooperation give estimates but use abstract paradigms. Role-playing games give agreements but mostly no data. The large-basin simulations model many jurisdictions, whereas Camaiore’s conflict is between users inside one municipality, over flood risk and retention. No game in the library models a Contratto di Fiume itself: forming a contract, testing it against adverse events and handing it to people who did not write it.

A bespoke game can be that rehearsal space. Its stress tests are social-preference dilemmas that are well researched one by one (common pool, public good with a threshold, time preference), so results can be read against known baselines. One rule set played at two scales, tables of about 4 and a digital group of about 30, serves quantitative research. Published as an open board game, it serves dissemination.

This is our argument. The sources show that the ingredients exist and where the gaps are. They do not show that this game will work, that the gap claim is complete (it rests on our own search), or that games of this kind change field behaviour. The dual role (instrument and dissemination artefact) is argued in The game serves as instrument and as dissemination artefact; the question of scale in Large online groups are where the question bites; the combination of dilemmas in Combining dilemmas is justified by coopetition.

Arguments

The place and the instrument

  1. The place sets the problem. The Camaiore basin roughly coincides with the municipality, so the vertical conflict is between users (hill landowners, plain, coast and tourism), not between jurisdictions. Flood risk dominates and coastal water quality is a second upstream-downstream externality, so the game’s resource is flood-risk reduction and retention, not water allocation (Game Experiments for Contract-Making in Watersheds, Camaiore context table). The large-basin games (Murray-Darling, Syr Darya, Magdalena-Cauca) model many jurisdictions; only their mechanisms transfer, not scale or calibration (Game Experiments for Contract-Making in Watersheds, inference 8 and Open).
  2. The field is a slow testbed. No signed or active Contratto di Fiume for Camaiore has been found; this needs checking (Game Experiments for Contract-Making in Watersheds, claim 1). Elsewhere the instrument is slow: the Aniene process ran about 20 years from the first forum to the last plenary, about 80 of about 200 started processes are signed (self-reported), the Aniene articles read contain no sanction clause, and no independent evaluation of results is in the library (Contratti di Fiume, claims 14, 16, 21 and Limits). Expansion basins were still incomplete 25 years after the 1996 flood (Game Experiments for Contract-Making in Watersheds, Camaiore context table). Our inference: how a contract responds to a flood or drought can hardly be observed in the field on a thesis timescale, so play is a practical way to see its clauses under stress. Signature is too slow an outcome; behaviour in the process is the earlier measure (Contratti di Fiume, inference 7).
  3. A Contratto di Fiume has a structure a game can mirror. It runs on a fixed sequence of documents, a Programma d’Azione of about three years with obligations per actor, a five-year term with renewal after monitoring, and free entry and exit (Contratti di Fiume, claims 9, 11, 14). Entry, exit and renewal are the real-world handover clauses (Contratti di Fiume, inference 8). The instrument’s own quality criteria ask for balanced dialogue and monitoring of the quality of participation (Contratti di Fiume, claims 10 and 11), so measuring whether deliberation drifts towards the strong makes the game part of the process, not external to it (Contratti di Fiume, inference 4).

What earlier watershed games leave open

  1. Controlled games give estimates, but abstract ones. Location shapes provision, reciprocity and trust drive upstream-downstream cooperation, downstream distrust may limit it, asymmetric appropriation reduced cooperation, removing enforcement was followed by a response consistent with crowding out, and non-binding contracts were hard to sustain in the Syr Darya game (Game Experiments for Contract-Making in Watersheds, claims 2 to 5). These are a learning set for clause design: what downstream players can observe, and whether a clause is binding or soft (Game Experiments for Contract-Making in Watersheds, inferences 1 and 2). None of them plays a contract as a bundle of clauses.
  2. Role-playing games give rules, but little data. Wat-A-Game, the River Basin Game, Lingmuteychu and the POMCA game aim at dialogue and agreements. The River Basin Game has no scoring and captures outcomes through discussion, notes and video, and only it and Lingmuteychu report institutions created after play (Collection of Watershed Games, design patterns and game 3). The evidence that such games change real outcomes rests on case reports (Game Experiments for Contract-Making in Watersheds, claims 10 and 13, inference 7).
  3. Neither family tests a contract against shocks or hands it over. This is our synthesis of arguments 4 and 5, not a finding of any source. The library holds no experimental game used inside an Italian Contratto di Fiume; this comes from our own search of the critical Italian literature (Game Experiments for Contract-Making in Watersheds, Open).
  4. Earlier games supply patterns to reuse. Upstream-downstream asymmetry as the core mechanic (Wat-A-Game, River Basin Game, Lingmuteychu); an individual phase followed by a collective one; role swapping, as in the River Basin Game and the third Lingmuteychu session, a first handover-like mechanism; adverse events after each round (Facing Floods news events, POMCA challenge scenarios, floods and droughts in Lords of the Valley, wet and dry years in the River Basin Game); and SimBasin’s indicators, which show the realised value and a band of possible values so players can tell good management from luck (Collection of Watershed Games, design patterns and games 3, 5, 7, 9, 11). The last matters for a stress test, where luck and contract quality must be separable. A two-phase design, a controlled phase followed by a context-rich one, is reported by Bartels et al. (2022), which has no source note (Game Experiments for Contract-Making in Watersheds, Limits).

Why the dilemmas, and why the events

  1. Known dilemmas make usable stress modules. Each comes with baselines and known levers.

    • Common pool: a review of 123 CPR game studies (Group Size in Common-Pool Resource Games, claim 2).
    • Public good with a threshold: a known threshold can turn the game into coordination, uncertainty about its location turns it back, and communication raises success (Collective Risk, Thresholds and Tipping Points, claims 1 to 5).
    • Cooperation with a successor: individual extraction almost always destroys the pool and a binding vote sustained all pools (Cooperation with Future Generations, claims 2 and 3). Players also give more to cheap short-term than to costly long-term measures (Cooperation with Future Generations, claim 7).
    • Preference scales exist as covariates (Measuring Social Preferences, claims 1 to 4).

    These games predict field behaviour poorly (Measuring Social Preferences, claim 5; External Validity of Experimental Games, claim 1). The case for them is therefore baselines and comparative statics, not prediction.

  2. Adverse events are the stress, so they should load the contract. A severe-but-rare exogenous risk left contributions unchanged in a large online public goods game, while uncertain tipping points made coordination harder and destruction likelier (Collective Risk, Thresholds and Tipping Points, claims 5 and 7). Our inference: an event should not be expected to raise cooperation by threat alone. Its job is to realise a loss that the contract’s clauses distribute, such as extraction rules, risk-pooling and research funding, and to show whether the contract holds. What players know about an event is itself an instrument (Collective Risk, Thresholds and Tipping Points, inference 1).

  3. A bespoke design fixes framing, payment and board as design choices. Treatments, remuneration and the game board changed behaviour in a framed field experiment (Games as Interventional and Participatory Research Tools, claim 5). Validity can be designed in by framing the game in the focal institution, asking what players thought it was about, and expecting direction of effects to travel better than size (External Validity of Experimental Games, claim 6, inferences 2 and 3). A Contratto di Fiume is that focal institution, which an abstract paradigm cannot mimic. Abstraction is costly and needs an explicit framework (Games as Interventional and Participatory Research Tools, claim 4), so building our own means doing that work in the open.

  4. Contribution to game experimental economics (our inference). The object of study becomes a contract of several clauses and its inheritance. The institution studies in the library test one institution at a time, whether chosen or imposed (Endogenous Institutions and Self-Governance in Commons, claims 1 to 4), and the generational study passes on a resource (Cooperation with Future Generations, claims 1 to 3). A review of CPR games calls for large-scale international research initiatives (External Validity of Experimental Games, claim 5); an openly published rule set and platform is one way to let other basins replicate.

One system, two scales, one open format

  1. One rule set at two group sizes. Most size manipulations in CPR games find no effect, and the sizes studied run from 2 to 8 (Group Size in Common-Pool Resource Games, claims 2 and 3). Tables of about 4 sit inside that range and are comparable with the literature. A digital group of about 30 is outside it, with one online one-shot public goods study as the only precedent (Group Size in Common-Pool Resource Games, claim 8, inference 2). Playing the same rules at both sizes is therefore itself a test of scale. Perceived size may matter more than headcount, so what the interface shows about the number of players is a design lever (Group Size in Common-Pool Resource Games, claims 4 and 5). Participatory games already span these sizes: Facing Floods with 5 players, POMCA groups of up to 10, Lords of the Valley with 15 to 25, the River Basin Game with 30 to 35 (Collection of Watershed Games, summary matrix). Wat-A-Game has an analogue form and a NetLogo version where computer agents can replace players, a precedent for one rule set on two platforms (Collection of Watershed Games, game 1).
  2. An open board game has precedent as a dissemination format. Facing Floods is a printable A4/A3 board under CC BY-NC-SA; the River Basin Game’s example budget is about US$500 for 50 people; Get the Grade is a table game; Wat-A-Game ships a blank kit for building one’s own (Collection of Watershed Games, games 1, 3, 7, 10). Evidence that serious games change behaviour is thin, so the board game can be justified as an output without claiming a proven effect (Games as Interventional and Participatory Research Tools, claims 6 to 8, inference 4).

Open

  • The gap claim is ours and thinly sourced. The collection note has no source notes behind it, and Facing Floods, Virtual River, Lords of the Valley and Get the Grade have no citable publication yet. Most other games were read from abstracts and catalogue records (Game Experiments for Contract-Making in Watersheds, Limits and Open). Check before the text says that no game does this.
  • Inheritance of institutions. The proposal says this has not been tested experimentally. That is not established in the library and needs a literature check (Cooperation with Future Generations, inference 5).
  • Time preference has no source as a dilemma. The design mechanic of securing points early or risking more later is not supported by any note. The nearest material is the short-term versus long-term cost finding and the impatience measure in fishermen, which did not predict field behaviour (Cooperation with Future Generations, claim 7; External Validity of Experimental Games, claim 2). See also Combining dilemmas is justified by coopetition.
  • Roles do not map cleanly across scales. The current proposal uses four zones with a company and a villager role each, which is eight roles. A table of about 4 and a group of about 30 divide neither by 4 nor by 8 evenly. Does a table of 4 mean one player per zone, and what do 30 players do? Large online groups are where the question bites records that earlier design notes used six players per table.
  • Which version yields confirmatory data? The digital game pays points to cash. A board version for dissemination is unlikely to be paid, and payment is a design variable. Whether the two versions give comparable data is untested (Games as Interventional and Participatory Research Tools, Open; The game serves as instrument and as dissemination artefact, Open).
  • Analogue constraints. The proposal keeps extractions, investments and adverse-event risks private. In a board game this needs cards or envelopes and caps complexity. How much of the digital rule set survives is open.
  • An outside-the-game measure. Validity is weak or mixed, so an observable real-world outcome should be planned from the start (External Validity of Experimental Games, Open; Game Experiments for Contract-Making in Watersheds, Open).
  • Camaiore access. Status of any Contratto di Fiume or “Verso un CdF” process, and an introduction to the Municipality, the Consorzio di Bonifica and local stakeholders, are not yet in hand (Contratti di Fiume, Open). Whether real stakeholders play or proxies do is undecided.
  • Licence and ownership. Which open licence the board game gets, and who holds the rights given the host institution, is undecided. The Wat-A-Game licensing terms are still to verify (Game Experiments for Contract-Making in Watersheds, Open).
  • Wording. Describe the participatory family as policy constructed through a participatory or interventional approach.
