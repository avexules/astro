[!Doctoral Program in Social Sciences for Sustainability and Wellbeing (S3W)] Title: Cooperating with Distant Others: Can Downstream Voice Help Sustain Shared Watersheds under Collective Risk?
Keywords: interregional cooperation; common pool resource; public good collective-risk threshold; combined dilemma; downstream voice

1. Motivation and Relevance of the Research

Tuscany’s productive economy faces a double transition: adapting to intensifying ecological stress while building a “new welfare”. Both are structurally similar to an intergenerational dilemma, but they also appear within a single generation when resource use is spatially separated. Water streams make it clear: upstream users extract, dam, or pollute a shared watershed; downstream users bear the costs and cannot reciprocate or retaliate (Janssen et al. 2011). Economic games can model the interregional dilemmas that arise when current decisions affect the availability of shared resources for distant others. The pressure is compounded by rising inequality and by uncertain ecological tipping points (Maas et al., 2017), which together make some form of new welfare and adaptation unavoidable. Against this background, the behavioural challenges show: pro-social contribution is driven far more by local, regional motives than by interregional ones. In addition, regional distance and poor coordination actively inhibit cooperation that might otherwise exist (Robst et al., 2006). A regional agenda for resilience and ecological transition cannot be delivered if distance is disregarded. This is where sustainability and wellbeing meet: keeping the shared productive stock healthy across regions, regenerating it, and investing enough to stay clear of catastrophic thresholds; and the wellbeing owed to the communities who receive whatever is left of the resource. The climate-change social dilemma is treated as a first-order strategic problem well beyond economics: management scholarship reviews it systematically and builds its solution taxonomy on the climate-action agendas of the UN, IPCC, World Bank and WEF (Rashidi-Sabet et al., 2022). To test these strategic perspectives on a behavioural level, I see a need for understanding combined dilemmas and richer spatial structures, hence my motivation for an economic game study on interregional cooperation with a present downstream group and combined common pool resource extraction with a public good collective-risk threshold. The societal challenge and the knowledge gap targeted is whether an institution can deliberately support sustained cooperation between users who are structurally unable to reciprocate with each other.

2. Literature Review and Theoretical Background

The project draws together three literatures. First, the experimental study of common pool resource games with communication establishes both the problem and the levers: giving a disadvantaged party a communication channel changes extraction and investment behaviour even without enforcement power, and risky or uncertain environments interact with that channel in non-trivial ways (Janssen et al. 2011). More directly, seating a downstream voice (in the form of communication- or veto-rights) in the room should shift sharing toward fairness, so that coordinated play must hold across regions rather than within one location alone. Second, the collective risk social dilemma supplies the stakes: when a shared resource has an uncertain tipping point, groups defect not only from greed but from an inability to coordinate on a safe ceiling (Maas et al., 2017). Layering it with unequal endowments reproduces the structural conditions of a transitioning regional economy, where upstream and downstream regions rarely start from the same baseline. For contextualising analyses, the Cooperative and Competitive Personality Scale (CCPS) by Xie, Yu, Chen & Chen (2006) is a valid measure of coopetition in social dilemma and can be useful to be taken into account as control variables alongside demographics (Lu et al., 2013). It acknowledges that cooperativeness and competitiveness are two independent dimensions within the behaviour of an individual (Brandenburger & Nalebuff, 1996; Bonta, 1997; Bengtsson & Kock, 2000), leaving dichotomous categorisations behind. In this setting, actors must compete to appropriate the common resource yet cooperate to invest in the public good and keep the collective-risk from triggering. Third, the nudge literature shows that incentive-neutral interventions can move long-term or distant-regarding contributions, but they are typically tested in isolation and mostly in temporal rather than spatial framings (Böhm et al., 2020). This exposes a gap, since we do not know whether upstream cooperation is transmissible along a supply chain of regions, whether a cooperative upstream region hands down a cooperative equilibrium to its downstream neighbour, or whether each regional link resets the game independently. The project positions itself as an experimental extension of Böhm et al. (2020) and Janssen et al. (2011) that embeds a downstream group inside a common pool resource game with a collective-risk public good, and compares institutional communication and veto against a private nudge.

3. Research Questions and Hypotheses

*Diagram 1: Resource flow/game loop; Diagram 2: Spatial structure (upstream/downstream)*

Primary research question. Does representation from the downstream group have an impact on the sustainability of shared resources in comparison to no direct interaction? Secondary questions. Does giving downstream players a voice (communication- or veto-rights) outperform an equivalent private nudge to upstream users? And is upstream cooperation transmissible along the chain to further downstream players, or does it reset at each link?

Hypotheses. (H1a) The experimental group veto will have significantly higher cooperation behaviour (extract less, donate more) than the communication group. (H1b) The experimental group communication will have significantly higher cooperation behaviour (extract less, donate more) than the nudge group. (H1c) The experimental group nudge will have significantly higher cooperation behaviour (extract less, donate more) than the control group. (H2) Cooperation is partially transmissible along the chain: a downstream group receiving a healthy stock cooperates more (donates more, extracts less) than one inheriting a depleted stock.

Expected contribution. Evidence on whether an institutional downstream voice beats a private nudge, and a first direct test of whether cooperation propagates to a next cohort rather than being re-earned.

4. Methodology and Research Design

Treatments. The study will consist of an experimental design with three different experimental groups and one control group, with every participant being assigned to one experimental group and the control (in a random order). The experimental groups are the following: a) veto: one participant in DS (downstream) is randomly selected to play a role in US (upstream) where they can cancel the extraction outcome for participants that extracted over a threshold chosen by the veto player before every round; b) communication: one participant in DS is randomly selected to play a role in US where they can send messages in the chat group; c) nudge: no direct interaction between US and DS players, but private feedback is shown privately to each player before confirming the extraction amount; d) control: no direct interaction between US and DS players.

Sample and data collection. Participants will be recruited mainly through trustworthy online platforms such as Prolific (Peer et al., 2021) and in laboratory settings where a lower participation is expected. The data will be directly collected through oTree, which is common to be used for the setup of such experiments (Bilancini et al., 2024). Every participant will be paid points-to-cash.

Analysis. Independent variables: veto, communication, nudge and control experimental groups. Dependent variables: extracted resources ($R_{e}$), invested resources ($R_{i}$), remaining common pool ($R_{c}$), mean individual holdings ($R_{\bar{h}}$) (for US and DS respectively). Statistical tools: ANOVA to check statistical differences between the four groups; Fisher F for overall group differences; Student t-Test for specific group differences of $R_{e},R_{i},R_{c},R_{\bar{h}}$; Ordinary Least Regression to check the statistical impact of the coefficients of the independent variables, alongside demographics and CCPS as control variables; $$ \begin{gathered}R_{e}=\alpha +{\beta }{1}\ast age+{\beta }{2}\ast gender+{\beta }{3}\ast CCPS+{\beta }{4}\ast veto+{\beta }{5}\ast comm.+{\beta }{6}\ast nudge+{\beta }{7}\ast control \ R{i}=\alpha +{\beta }{1}\ast age+{\beta }{2}\ast gender+{\beta }{3}\ast CCPS+{\beta }{4}\ast veto+{\beta }{5}\ast comm.+{\beta }{6}\ast nudge+{\beta }{7}\ast control \ R{e}=\alpha +{\beta }{1}\ast age+{\beta }{2}\ast gender+{\beta }{3}\ast CCPS+{\beta }{4}\ast veto+{\beta }{5}\ast comm.+{\beta }{6}\ast nudge+{\beta }{7}\ast control \ R{\bar{h}}=\alpha +{\beta }{1}\ast age+{\beta }{2}\ast gender+{\beta }{3}\ast CCPS+{\beta }{4}\ast veto+{\beta }{5}\ast comm.+{\beta }{6}\ast nudge+{\beta }_{7}\ast control\end{gathered} $$ Timeline. Participation involves informed consent, anonymised data, incentives without deception; the study will be pre-registered on the OSF platform. The work proceeds in phases in which consecutive publications are planned: [Year 1] experimental setup and pilot ( Paper 1); [Year 1/2] conducting the experiment and its data analysis ( Paper 2); [Year 3] defended dissertation with final contributions and dissemination through a published board game ( Paper 3).

5. Expected Impact and Contribution

The project delivers an experimental paradigm that puts an institutional downstream voice to a direct test and, for the first time, compares communication and veto against a private nudge while measuring whether cooperation is transmissible along a spatial chain of regions (extending Böhm et al., 2020; Janssen et al., 2011). It thereby isolates a mechanism for creating interregional reciprocity where direct retaliation is structurally impossible. Validity is anchored in the economic-games literature (Pisor et al., 2020) and in games-for-conservation-conflict methodology (Redpath et al., 2018). For research institutions and regional planners, the results offer tested methods for interregional welfare-and-adaptation policy: whether communication- or veto-rights for downstream players outperforms a cheaper private nudge, and whether a cooperative cohort transmits its equilibrium onward. The experiment supplies behavioural inference for sustainability-governance that the climate social-dilemma literature has already identified at the strategic level: binding regulation (veto), non-binding representation (communication), and informational feedback (nudge) tested against control (Rashidi-Sabet et al., 2022). The paradigm may also be transferable to a temporal perspective, e.g. for present/future generation trade-offs. In the context of Tuscany, these findings can provide behavioural evidence for designing institutions that strengthen cooperation across spatially connected watersheds under increasing ecological pressure, thereby supporting both ecological transition and the development of a new welfare model. Results will additionally be disseminated as a serious game for learning and stakeholder engagement, e.g. in cooperation with the Game Science Research Center (see BLUTUBE; Di Paolo & Pizziol, 2024), turning the method into a playable artefact for the public.
