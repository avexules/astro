[!Doctoral Program in Social Sciences for Sustainability and Wellbeing (S3W)] Title: Co-Constructing Policy: Real- and Proxy-Stakeholders in a Watershed Dilemma
Thematic: “Territorial well-being and sustainability through the enhancement of the water system and river park between the Apuan Alps, streams, and coast in the Municipality of Camaiore.”
Keywords: participatory policy-construction; common pool resource game; adverse events; cultural theory worldviews; watershed governance

1. Motivation and Relevance of the Research

Water governance in the tuscan Camaiore basin has become urgent: intensifying seasonal drought and flood cycles stress a watershed that runs directionally from the Apuan Alps, down through the streams, to the coast. This directionality is a core problem. Upstream extraction and pollution impose costs downstream that the coast cannot reciprocate. Each zone along the gradient holds an asymmetric role and stake, yet periodical adverse events sit over all of them, so no zone is safe from the risk of losing individual holdings. However, conventional, top-down water plans tend to fail precisely because this non-reciprocity leaves downstream actors without leverage and upstream actors with no incentive to take responsibility for the costs they cause. Therefore, the governance challenge is not merely technical but distributive and institutional: how can asymmetrical stakeholders agree on and sustainably own a shared policy? This sits at the core of sustainability and wellbeing. The study is about the health and resilience of the basin’s common pool under climatic stress and the territorial meaning the residents attach to their river system. The practice gap, this project addresses, is that policies and contracts for such basins are typically prescribed rather than authored by those they bind. Following the approach that stakeholders should explore and shape the policy space themselves (Redpath et al., 2018; Ostrom, 1990), the project asks what happens when the zones are given the tools to author their own watershed contract before the municipality commits to one.

2. Literature Review and Theoretical Background

The project draws together three literatures: First, common-pool resource (CPR) theory establishes that resource users can self-govern through their own institutions rather than external imposition (Ostrom, 1990). Here the watershed is modelled as a CPR game whose zones form asymmetrical subgroups; players negotiate for policies that becomes an emergent contract. Second, work on public good games show that severe adverse events do not change cooperation significantly (Bilancini et al., 2024). Although, when tipping points are uncertain, coordination for a public good is harder and destruction more likely (Maas et al., 2017), while communication and shared information help groups steer toward the threshold (Tavoni et al., 2011; Balliet, 2010). Accordingly, the project investigates further the effect of adverse events in a CPR game. Third, Cultural Theory’s four ways of organising – hierarchy, individualism, egalitarianism, fatalism – are analysed as worldviews that shape CPR play (Thompson et al., 1990; Bednarik et al., 2019); next to the mean worldview of each group, each policy is categorised in one of the four worldviews. So far, worldview analysis has been applied to behaviour, not to the policy that stakeholders negotiate. Moreover, policy-construction in social dilemmas like common pool resource games with uncertain adverse events are rarely run participatorily, therefore, this project explores that for a river contract rather than studying a pre-designed solution.

3. Research Questions and Objectives

*Diagram 1: Resource flow/regular round loop; Diagram 2: Interval game loop*

Primary research question. Which water-governance policies do stakeholders co-construct when they occupy asymmetric watershed roles under periodic, uncertain adverse events? Secondary questions. Which policies correlate with which game outcomes, and how does the worldview each players bring shape the contract the group converges on?

Objectives. Formalise the Camaiore basin as an asymmetric CPR game under periodic uncertain adverse events; Design of twelve policies (three dimensions * four worldviews) based on the prototypes below; run participatory sessions in which stakeholders create a river contract out of policies; conduct the data analysis; disseminate the method in form of a published serious game.

Hypotheses. Sessions with predominantly egalitarian policies will have less extracted resources than sessions with other policies. Sessions with predominantly individualist policies will have more extracted resources than sessions with other policies. Sessions with predominantly hierarchical policies will have higher inequality in final individual holdings. Sessions with predominantly fatalistic policies will have lower inequality in final individual holdings.

4. Methodology and Research Design

I propose an non-experimental, observational using an exploratory game whose output is a co-constructed watershed contract consisting of up to three policies authored by the group. Each group is composed of eight asymmetrical roles:

Zone asymmetry. Extraction occurs in the order of upstream to downstream: first mountain, hills, city and finally coast. Company roles can propose policies in their respective policy round. Villager and company roles can vote for the propositions. Roles are endowed asymmetrically in the beginning.

Baseline rules. Extractions are private. Investments are private. Adverse events and their risks are private.

Contracts. The active contract consists of up to three policies: one extraction governance, one risk-pooling and one research-funding policy. This means, only a single policy per dimension can be active at a time. Policy rounds. In an upstream policy round, the company roles of the mountain- and hill-zone propose two policies from the policy collection. All players vote to decide for one policy that will be active (until it may get replaced with another policy in the same dimension). Draws in the voting are resolved at random. Upstream and downstream policy rounds alternate. There are 4 policy rounds in total.

Policy prototypes. Extraction governance policies control extraction limits and procedures of sanctions for overextraction. Risk-pooling policies control the collection of funds for insurances against adverse events. Research-funding policies control the collection of funds to gain information on the otherwise unknown next adverse event.

Sample and data collection. Roles can be played by real stakeholders, or their stakes can be adopted as a role-play by proxy stakeholders. Proxy stakeholders will be recruited mainly through trustworthy online platforms such as Prolific (Peer et al., 2021). Real stakeholders may play in laboratory settings. The data will be directly collected through oTree, which is common to be used for the setup of such experiments (Bilancini et al., 2024). Every participant will be paid points-to-cash.

Analysis. Ordinary least squares regression to check the statistical impact of the coefficients of the independent variables, alongside demographics and group mean worldview as control variables. Group mean worldviews and demographics are determined in a survey beforehand using established measurements (Bednarik et al., 2019; Lima & Castro, 2005, Thompson et al., 1990). Timeline. Participation involves informed consent, incentives without deception and anonymised data, with particular care for real-stakeholder identities and for the local-political sensitivity of intervening in a real municipal issue. The work proceeds in phases in which consecutive publications are planned: [Year 1] game design and pilot ( Paper 1); [Year 1/2] sessions, coding and data analysis ( Paper 2); [Year 3] defended dissertation with final contributions back to the municipality and dissemination through a published board game ( Paper 3).

5. Expected Impact and Contribution

The project delivers the first participatory watershed game that reads the constructed policy through the four ways of organizing. It offers evidence on how upstream–downstream asymmetry shapes the resulting water contract, and on what policies help coordinate against repeated adverse events. Validity is anchored in the economic games literature (Pisor et al., 2020) and the games-for-conservation-conflict literature (Redpath et al., 2018): by modelling the policies in design and regulation of the game, it aims for higher ecological and construct validity than abstract commons paradigms achieve. The project yields a transferable method for the Municipality of Camaiore, allowing stakeholders to explore and author watershed policies before committing to them. Options can be stress-tested against adverse events, and the robustness of contracts can be examined in play rather than in the field. The employed game system will additionally be disseminated as a serious game for learning and stakeholder engagement, e.g. in cooperation with the Game Science Research Center (see BLUTUBE; Di Paolo & Pizziol, 2024), turning the method into a playable artefact for the public.
