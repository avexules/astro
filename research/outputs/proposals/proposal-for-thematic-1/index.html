[!Doctoral Program in Social Sciences for Sustainability and Wellbeing (S3W)] Title: Cooperating with Future Generations: Can their Voice Help Sustain Commons under Collective Risk?
Thematic: “Socio-economic resilience and ecological transition: strategies for productive adaptation and new welfare in Tuscany.”
Keywords: intergenerational cooperation; common pool resource game; public good collective-risk threshold; combined dilemma; future generation voice

1. Motivation and Relevance of the Research

Tuscany’s productive economy faces a double transition: adapting to intensifying ecological stress while building a “new welfare”. Both are fundamentally intergenerational problems. Economic games can model the intergenerational dilemmas that arise when current decisions affect the availability of shared resources for future generations. Cohorts over-extract common resources because the benefits are theirs while the costs fall on successors who can neither reciprocate nor retaliate. Cooperation with the future is therefore structurally unenforceable (Hauser et al., 2014). The pressure is compounded by rising inequality and by uncertain ecological tipping points (Maas et al., 2017), which together make some form of new welfare and adaptation unavoidable. Against this background, the behavioural challenges show: pro-social contribution is driven far more by short-term, intragenerational motives than by long-term, intergenerational ones. In addition, intragenerational conflict and poor coordination actively inhibit cooperation with the future that might otherwise exist (Bayle et al., 2026). A regional agenda for resilience and ecological transition cannot be delivered if future generations are disregarded. This is where sustainability and wellbeing meet: keeping the shared productive stock healthy across generations, regenerating it and investing enough to stay clear of catastrophic thresholds; and the new welfare owed to successors who inherit the economy we leave them. The shared productive stock stands in for a regional common resource in the ecological transition e.g. a circular-economy material pool or a shared carbon budget. The climate-change social dilemma is treated as a first-order strategic problem well beyond economics: management scholarship reviews it systematically and builds its solution taxonomy on the climate-action agendas of the UN, IPCC, World Bank and WEF (Rashidi-Sabet et al., 2022). To test these strategic perspectives on a behavioural level, I see a need for understanding combined dilemmas and richer social structures, hence my motivation for an economic game study on intergenerational cooperation with a present future generation and combined common pool resource extraction with a public good collective risk-threshold. The societal challenge and the knowledge gap targeted, is whether an institution can deliberately support a sustained cooperation that outlives the very generation that designs it.

2. Literature Review and Theoretical Background

The project draws together three literatures. First, the experimental study of cooperating with the future establishes both the problem and the levers. Two largely independent handles raise intergenerational cooperation: giving present actors a stake in the future, and cues of population continuity or density (Chang et al., 2021). More directly, seating a future generation voice (in the form of communication- or veto-rights) in the room shifts sharing toward fairness, so that coordinated play must hold across cohorts (Farjam & Wolf, 2021). Second, the collective risk social dilemma supplies the stakes: when a shared resource has an uncertain tipping point, groups defect not only from greed but from an inability to coordinate (Maas et al., 2017). Layering it with unequal endowments reproduces the structural conditions of a transitioning regional economy. For contextualising analyses, Social Value Orientation (SVO) is an established measure to predict cooperation in game theory dilemma and can be useful to be taken into account as control variables alongside demographics (Balliet et al., 2009). Third, the nudge literature shows that incentive-neutral interventions can move long-term contributions, but they are typically tested in isolation (Böhm et al., 2020). This exposes the gap that we do not know whether intragenerational cooperation is inheritable, whether a cooperative cohort hands down a cooperative equilibrium, or each generation resets. The project positions itself accordingly: an experimental extension of Böhm et al. (2020) and Hauser et al. (2014) that embeds the future generation inside an overlapping-generations common pool resource game and compares institutional communication and veto against a private nudge (Farjam & Wolf, 2021).

3. Research Questions and Hypotheses

*Diagram 1: Resource flow/game loop; Diagram 2: Intergenerational structure*

Primary research question. Does representation from future generation have an impact on the sustainability of shared resources in comparison to no direct interaction? Secondary questions. Does giving the future a voice (communication- or veto-rights) outperform an equivalent private nudge? And is cooperation inheritable, transmitted to successors, or it changes substantially for each generation?

Hypotheses. (H1a) The experimental group veto will have significantly higher cooperation behaviour (extract less, donate more) than the communication group. (H1b) The experimental group communication will have significantly higher cooperation behaviour (extract less, donate more) than the nudge group. (H1c) The experimental group nudge will have significantly higher cooperation behaviour (extract less, donate more) than the control group. (H2) Cooperation is partially inheritable: a successor inheriting a healthy stock cooperates more (donates more, extracts less) than one inheriting a depleted stock.

Expected contribution. Evidence on whether an institutional future generation voice beats a private nudge, and a first direct test of whether cooperation is inherited across cohorts rather than re-earned each generation.

4. Methodology and Research Design

Treatments. The study will consist of an experimental design with three different experimental groups and one control group, with every participant being assigned to one experimental group and the control (in a random order). The experimental groups are the following: a) veto: one participant in G2 (generation 2) is randomly selected to play a role in G1 (generation 1) where they can cancel the extraction outcome for participants that extracted over a threshold chosen by the veto player before every round; b) communication: one participant in G2 is randomly selected to play a role in G1 where they can send messages in the chat group; c) nudge: no direct interaction between G1 and G2 players, but private feedback is shown privately to each player before confirming the extraction amount; d) control: no direct interaction between G1 and G2 players.

Sample and data collection. Participants will be recruited mainly through trustworthy online platforms such as Prolific (Peer et al., 2021) and in laboratory settings where a lower participation is expected. The data will be directly collected through oTree, which is common to be used for the setup of such experiments (Bilancini et al., 2024). Every participant will be paid points-to-cash.

Analysis. IV: veto, communication, nudge and control experimental groups. Timeline. Participation involves informed consent, anonymised data, incentives without deception; the study will be pre-registered on the OSF platform. The work proceeds in phases in which consecutive publications are planned: [Year 1] experimental setup and pilot ( Paper 1); [Year 1/2] conducting the experiment and its data analysis ( Paper 2); [Year 3] defended dissertation with final contributions and dissemination through a published board game ( Paper 3).

5. Expected Impact and Contribution

The project delivers an experimental paradigm that puts an institutional future generation to a direct test and, for the first time, compares communication and veto against a private nudge while measuring whether cooperation is inherited across cohorts (extending Böhm et al., 2020; Hauser et al., 2014). It thereby isolates a mechanism for creating intergenerational reciprocity. Validity is anchored in the economic-games literature (Pisor et al., 2020) and in games-for-conservation-conflict methodology (Redpath et al., 2018). For research institutions and regional planners, the results offer tested methods for intergenerational welfare-and-adaptation policy: whether communication- or veto-rights for the future outperforms a cheaper private nudge, and whether a cooperative cohort transmits its equilibrium onward. The experiment supplies behavioural inference for sustainability-governance that the climate social-dilemma literature has already identified at the strategic level: binding regulation (veto), non-binding representation (communication), and informational feedback (nudge) tested against control (Rashidi-Sabet et al., 2022). The future generation-paradigm may also be transferable to other resource systems, e.g. for directional watersheds with upstream/downstream extraction. Results will additionally be disseminated as a serious game for learning and stakeholder engagement, e.g. in cooperation with the Game Science Research Center (see BLUTUBE; Di Paolo & Pizziol, 2024), turning the method into a playable artefact for the public.
