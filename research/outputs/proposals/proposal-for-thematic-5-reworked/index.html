[!Doctoral Program in Social Sciences for Sustainability and Wellbeing (S3W)] Title: Does a Watershed Contract Survive its Authors? Constructing and Inheriting Policy in the Camaiore Basin
Thematic: “Territorial well-being and sustainability through the enhancement of the water system and river park between the Apuan Alps, streams, and coast in the Municipality of Camaiore.”
Keywords: contratto di fiume; participatory policy-construction; institutional inheritance; common pool resource game; cultural theory worldviews

1. Motivation and Relevance of the Research

The Camaiore basin runs from the Apuan Alps through the hills and the town to the coast, and intensifying drought and flood cycles stress it along its whole length. It is asymmetric in two ways at once. Positionally, extraction follows the flow: mountain users act first, and the costs of upstream extraction and pollution fall on downstream users who cannot reciprocate. Economically, starting endowments run the other way and rise from the mountains towards the coast. Those who act first hold least; those who bear the consequences hold most. Adverse events cut across all zones.

Top-down water plans tend to fail in such a setting because the actors they bind did not author them: downstream users lack leverage, and upstream users lack an incentive to own the costs they cause. Italian law already offers a participatory alternative, the Contratto di Fiume (Art. 68-bis, D.Lgs. 152/2006), a voluntary instrument negotiated among basin stakeholders. What practice leaves open is what happens after the negotiation. A contract that works for the group that wrote it must still govern those who come later: new stakeholders, new officeholders, new residents.

This is where sustainability and wellbeing meet. The health and resilience of the basin’s common pool under climatic stress, and the territorial meaning residents attach to their river, depend on rules that outlast the table at which they were agreed. The project answers the call in two steps. It lets the zones author their own watershed contract in play, before the Municipality commits to one (Redpath et al., 2018), and then tests whether successors who did not write it keep, defend and obey it.

2. Literature Review and Theoretical Background

Three findings and one gap structure the project. First, groups can build their own rules: resource users self-govern through institutions they author rather than ones imposed from outside (Ostrom, 1990). Second, chosen rules outperform imposed rules: the effect of a policy on cooperation was greater when subjects chose it democratically than when it was exogenously imposed, with selection effects controlled (Dal Bó et al., 2010). Third, groups pass on resources: cooperation towards successors who cannot reciprocate can be sustained across generations of players (Hauser et al., 2014).

The gap lies between the second and third findings: how do groups pass on rules? A rule may outperform an imposed one for the group that chose it, but it is unclear whether it keeps that advantage when inherited by a group that did not. To my knowledge, the inheritance of institutions, as opposed to resources, has not been tested experimentally.

Two further literatures supply the reading frame and the stakes. Cultural Theory’s four ways of organising (hierarchy, individualism, egalitarianism, fatalism) offer a vocabulary for what groups build (Thompson et al., 1990). They have so far been applied to players’ behaviour in commons games (Bednarik et al., 2019), not to the rules players negotiate. Here each policy prototype stands for one worldview, and the contracts constructed by groups are read on the grid × group plane. The stakes come from periodic, uncertain adverse events. Uncertain tipping points make coordination harder and destruction more likely (Maas et al., 2017), while the mere threat of severe adverse events did not change cooperation in public goods games (Bilancini et al., 2024). The adverse events in the game therefore give each contract something to be tested against, through its risk-pooling and research-funding policies.

Finally, participatory policy-construction in such dilemmas is rarely attempted, although games can serve as tools to address conflicts of this kind (Redpath et al., 2018).

3. Research Questions and Objectives

Primary research question. Does a watershed contract survive its authors?

Secondary questions. Which contracts do stakeholders construct when they occupy asymmetric watershed roles under periodic, uncertain adverse events (Stage 1)? Do successors who inherit a contract keep it, defend it and obey it, and does this depend on who is said to have written it and on the worldview its extraction rules express (Stage 2)?

Hypotheses (Stage 2).

  • H1 Retention: successors keep the inherited contract.
  • H2 Enforcement: successors defend it, measured as willingness to pay to sanction a violator.
  • H3 Compliance: successors obey it, measured as deviation from the contract’s benchmark.

The independent variables are contract provenance (inherited vs. neutral) and extraction governance (four worldviews); Stage 1 is descriptive.

Objectives. (1) Formalise the Camaiore basin as an asymmetric common pool resource game under adverse events. (2) Design twelve policy prototypes (three dimensions × four worldviews). (3) Observe which contracts stakeholders build. (4) Test whether those contracts survive their authors. (5) Hand the method over to the municipality and publish it as a serious game.

4. Methodology and Research Design

I propose a two-stage study with a bespoke economic game, developed as a research instrument in my work at the DE:HIVE Game Hub of HTW Berlin.

Game. Players occupy roles in four zones, in extraction order: Mountains, Hills, Town, Coast. Each zone has a company role and a villager role, and endowments are asymmetric. Extractions, investments and adverse events with their risks are private. A contract holds up to three policies, one per dimension, each drawn from four worldview prototypes:

Dimension Egalitarian Hierarchical Individualist Fatalist
Extraction governance Consensus quota, public extractions, consensus exclusion Manager-set quota, public extractions, graduated sanctions Individual quota, no sanction Random external quota, no sanction
Risk-pooling Mandatory equal pool, equal payout Central insurance, zone-tiered payouts No pool, optional bilateral insurance No insurance, losses absorbed individually
Research funding Jointly funded, public information Authority-led, private information Privately purchased, individually owned No research allowed

Stage 1 (descriptive). Cohort A (N = 40) creates the contract over eight extraction rounds (E1–E8) interleaved with four policy rounds: P1 sets extraction governance (E1, E2), P2 risk-pooling (E3, E4), P3 research funding (E5, E6), and P4 may re-author any dimension (E7, E8). Each policy round is an approval vote on the four variants and the status quo. The output is a final contract and a deliberation protocol, which are read on grid × group.

Stage 2 (experimental). Cohort B (N = 160) inherits a contract in a 2 × 4 between-subjects design with N = 20 per cell. Provenance is either inherited (“written by a previous group”, with its deliberation protocol) or neutral (“this contract is in force”). Extraction governance takes each of the four worldview prototypes. Risk-pooling and research funding are held constant. In Phase 1 (E1–E4) the contract cannot change, which isolates enforcement (H2) and compliance (H3). In Phase 2 (P1, E5, P2, E6, P3, E7, E8) an approval vote can change a policy on any dimension, which tests retention (H1).

Sample and data. Roles can be played by real stakeholders or adopted by proxy stakeholders, recruited mainly through trustworthy online platforms such as Prolific (Peer et al., 2021); real stakeholders may play in laboratory or municipal sessions. Data are collected with oTree (Bilancini et al., 2024), and participants are paid points-to-cash. Group-mean worldviews and demographics are measured beforehand (Bednarik et al., 2019; Lima & Castro, 2005; Thompson et al., 1990).

Analysis. Stage 2 outcomes are the final contract, willingness to pay to sanction a violator, and benchmark deviation. I estimate provenance, governance and their interaction by OLS (logit for retention), controlling for demographics and group-mean worldview.

Ethics and timeline. Participation involves informed consent, incentives without deception and anonymised data, with particular care for real-stakeholder identities and the political sensitivity of a live municipal issue. Stage 2 will be pre-registered on OSF. [Year 1] game design, pilot and Stage 1 (Paper 1); [Year 1/2] Stage 2 and analysis (Paper 2); [Year 3] dissertation, hand-over to the municipality and a published board game (Paper 3).

5. Expected Impact and Contribution

Scientific. The project offers the first test, to my knowledge, of whether an institution survives the group that authored it. It extends the finding that chosen rules outperform imposed ones (Dal Bó et al., 2010) and that groups pass on resources (Hauser et al., 2014) to the transmission of rules. It also reads a constructed policy, not only behaviour, through the four ways of organising.

Methodological. The game is a rehearsal space for a Contratto di Fiume and for how it is handed over. Validity is anchored in the economic-games literature (Pisor et al., 2020) and the games-for-conservation-conflict literature (Redpath et al., 2018), and modelling real policies in the game aims at higher ecological and construct validity than abstract commons paradigms.

Territorial. Camaiore can stress-test contracts in play against adverse events before committing in the field, and can see whether an agreement outlasts the table that made it. An introduction to the Municipality, the competent authorities and local stakeholders would be highly valuable, since it would allow real stakeholders to take part in Stage 1.

Dissemination. The game system will be published as a serious game for learning and stakeholder engagement, for example with the Game Science Research Center (see BLUTUBE; Di Paolo & Pizziol, 2024), turning the method into a playable artefact for the public.
