Transcript

Hello, my name is Jules Pommier, and I am very pleased to have the opportunity to give my presentation today. I’m an interdisciplinary researcher with a Bachelor’s and Master’s in Game Design and a teacher at the DE:HIVE Game Hub of HTW Berlin. Last year I was teaching at the first summer school of the European Game Science Lab, at IMT Lucca, together with Ennio Bilancini and Leonardo Boncinelli. The environment of the IMT and the occupation with design desiderata on sustainability and wellbeing sparked my interest in applying for this PhD programme.

What I do is both narrower and broader than what “game design” suggests: I develop games as a research method. In my master’s thesis, I designed a cognitive game to measure working-memory updating, developed with psychologists at the Max Planck and Leibniz Institutes. In a current project, I conduct research through a digital children’s book and a card game to observe their emotion regulation and mental health effects on children. For most of my life, the process of players re-negotiating game rules has fascinated me and made me want to research it.

That’s why I have submitted three closely related research topics, but I will present my favourite, on Thematic 5, which I have already continued to specify over the last month. The research runs in two stages and asks the following question: Does a watershed contract survive its authors after being inherited? Which brings me to the specific situation of Camaiore.

Camaiore is located at the foot of a short, steep watershed. Water runs in one direction: from the Apuan Alps, over the hills, through towns and down to the coast. These represent the four players in my economic game. The economy of these zones is roughly perpendicular to the water flow. Consequently, the coast has higher endowments than the mountains. To regulate extractions from the watershed, Italy has a tool called the Contratto di Fiume. But circumstances change. Here is the question nobody asks before signing such a contract: Does it remain valid and respected once it is inherited by successors?

Ostrom established that resource users can build the institutions that govern them. Experimental work since then shows that rules people choose outperform the same rules imposed on them. Hauser and colleagues showed what happens when one group hands a resource to the next. But nobody has asked what happens when a group passes on an institution …

Stage one is descriptive: what type of contract do the stakeholders create, and where is it positioned on the grid and group axes of Lima & Castros Cultural Theory? Stage two is experimental, and a contract may fail in one of three ways. I measure all three outcomes.

  • (H1) Groups shown the contract’s provenance retain more of it than groups shown no information about where it came from.
  • (H2) Provenance cohorts show higher willingness to pay to sanction violators.
  • (H3) Provenance cohorts deviate less from the contract-specific benchmark. The independent variables are the provenance of the contract, meaning whether it is inherited or communicated neutrally, and the contract itself, differing in the extraction governance. The dependent variables are the final re-authored contract, the willingness to pay to sanction a violator, and the deviation of the contract-specific benchmark.

Four players extract from a common pool and risk a high defect in its regeneration when overextracted, which is known as a collective risk. Extraction runs in downstream order, so what the mountain takes is physically unavailable to the coast. The stock regenerates after each round, but potential droughts reduce the regeneration rate of the common pool. Endowments run the other way: upstream zones extract first, but start with the least resources. Every extraction is public. Over the course of four policy rounds, the group votes on a three-policy contract on the grid & group dimensions of extraction governance, risk pooling and research for information about the collective-risk threshold. Voting is done by approval of Egalitarian, Hierarchical, Individualist or Fatalist variants of a policy, or keeping the status quo. After each round, anyone may pay two points to sanction a violator of the extraction quota.

In the experimental stage, a second cohort inherits the formed contracts. They receive one of the four real contracts created in the first stage, which differ only in terms of extraction governance. The other policy dimensions are held constant and are chosen by the median of stage 1. Half of the contracts are communicated as having been inherited from a predecessor group, including their deliberation protocol explaining their choices. The other half are simply informed that the contract is in force. In Phase 1, Cohort B plays four extraction rounds under the inherited contract. In Phase 2, they play three policy rounds between extractions in order to reauthorise.

On the scientific level, it is the first test of whether an institution survives the group that authored it. That extends the literature from how rules are chosen to whether they last. On the methodological level, it gives the opportunity to negotiate contracts in asymmetric economic environments, like for a Contratto di Fiume. On the territorial level, Camaiore gets to see which contracts hold and which decay, in play rather than in the field. The instrument would be most effective if a version could be run with actual stakeholders and authorities. I do not yet have that relationship, which is why this scholarship would be perfect.
