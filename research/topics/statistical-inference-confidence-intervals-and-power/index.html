Summary

The course route from describing a sample to drawing conclusions about a population: descriptive measures, correlation and regression, the sampling distribution, confidence intervals, significance tests and the two error types. It supplies the formulas and the assumptions behind them. For the PhD it matters because every confirmatory claim in a proposal needs a stated test, a sample size and a power argument, and because the course formulas assume simple random samples, which our designs will not satisfy.

Claims

  1. Variable type decides the calculation. Nominal and ordinal variables are categorical, interval and ratio are quantitative. Mode suits nominal data, median ordinal data, and mean interval and ratio data (Rooduijn and van Loon (Coursera), pages 45, 47).
  2. Spread. Sample variance is s² = Σ(x − x̄)² / (n − 1) and the standard deviation is its root. The interquartile range is Q3 − Q1 and is not affected by outliers, which are values beyond 1.5 IQR from the quartiles. The z-score z = (x − x̄) / s counts standard deviations from the mean, and z-scores sum to zero (Rooduijn and van Loon (Coursera), pages 47 to 49).
  3. Correlation and regression. Pearson’s r measures the direction and strength of a linear relation between −1 and +1 and is of no use for non-linear relations. The least-squares line ŷ = a + bx minimises the sum of squared residuals, with b = r · s_y / s_x and a = ȳ − b·x̄. r² is the share of variance in Y explained by X (Rooduijn and van Loon (Coursera), pages 50, 51).
  4. Correlation is not causation. A confounder (the example is genetics driving both chocolate consumption and body weight) can make a relation spurious. With few cases outliers weigh heavily (Rooduijn and van Loon (Coursera), pages 51, 52).
  5. Descriptive versus inferential statistics. Sample statistics use Roman letters (x̄, s) and population parameters Greek letters (μ, σ). Inference needs a representative sample, ideally a simple random one. Threats named: undercoverage, sampling bias, non-response bias and response bias (Rooduijn and van Loon (Coursera), page 79).
  6. Sampling distribution and the central limit theorem. The sampling distribution of the mean has mean μ and standard deviation σ/√n, and is approximately normal if the population is normal or the sample is large (n ≥ 30). For a proportion, σ_p = √(π(1 − π)/n), approximately normal if nπ ≥ 15 and n(1 − π) ≥ 15 (Rooduijn and van Loon (Coursera), pages 80, 81).
  7. Confidence interval for a mean. x̄ ± z·σ/√n, with z = 1.96 for 95%, 2.58 for 99% and 1.645 for 90%. When σ is unknown the sample s replaces it and the t-distribution (df = n − 1, thicker tails, close to normal at df ≈ 30) replaces z. Assumptions: a random sample and an approximately normal population, with the t-table robust against violations of normality and extreme outliers a warning sign. A higher confidence level costs precision (Rooduijn and van Loon (Coursera), pages 82 to 84).
  8. Confidence interval for a proportion. p ± z·se with se = √(p(1 − p)/n), valid with at least 15 successes and 15 failures (Rooduijn and van Loon (Coursera), page 84).
  9. Interpretation of an interval. With repeated sampling a 95% interval contains the population mean 95% of the time (Rooduijn and van Loon (Coursera), page 92).
  10. Sample size for estimation. For means n = σ²z²/m², for proportions n = p(1 − p)z²/m², where m is the margin of error. σ is unknown and needs an educated guess. For proportions p = 0.5 gives the largest n (Rooduijn and van Loon (Coursera), page 85).
  11. Significance test. H₀ states a specific parameter value and Hₐ a range of alternative values, mutually exclusive. H₀ is assumed true unless the evidence is convincing. The test statistic is the number of standard errors between the sample value and the H₀ value: z = (p − π₀)/se₀ with se₀ = √(π₀(1 − π₀)/n) for a proportion and t = (x̄ − μ₀)/(s/√n) for a mean. α is normally 0.05 with a two-tailed test. Not rejecting H₀ does not mean H₀ is true (Rooduijn and van Loon (Coursera), pages 88 to 91).
  12. Test and interval agree. If the two-tailed p-value is 0.05 or smaller, the 95% interval excludes the H₀ value (Rooduijn and van Loon (Coursera), page 92).
  13. Error types and power. A type I error (false positive) has probability α, a type II error (false negative) has probability β, and power is 1 − β. Lowering α raises β. Power helps determine the required n (Rooduijn and van Loon (Coursera), page 93).

Inferences

  1. Rounds are not independent observations. The formulas assume a random sample of independent cases (claims 7, 11). In a repeated game, rounds sit inside players, tables and timelines, so the effective sample is much smaller than the number of rounds. The course gives no tool for this. Clustered or multilevel models are a gap to fill before any confirmatory test is specified.
  2. A convenience sample changes what a p-value means. With an open call the randomisation assumption of claim 7 holds only for treatment assignment inside the sample. Inference then concerns the effect of the treatment on these participants, as in Experimental Design, Validity and Sampling (inference 5).
  3. The sample-size formulas do not answer the power question for treatment comparisons. Claim 10 gives sample size for estimating one mean or proportion in a simple random sample. A comparison of conditions in clustered data needs its own power calculation, probably by simulation. Claim 13 says power must be fixed in advance.
  4. Rare events break the proportion rule. If the outcome is a collapse of the shared resource, claim 8 requires at least 15 events and 15 non-events. Low event counts would call for another method or a continuous outcome.
  5. Report intervals next to tests. Claim 12 shows the interval carries the test’s information plus the precision. For a small expected effect the interval says what the data can and cannot rule out.
  6. State the tail of the test in advance. The course treats a two-tailed test as the norm (claim 11). A directional hypothesis can justify a one-tailed test, but only if declared before the data are seen (see Research Integrity, Pre-registration and Publication Bias).

Limits

  • The source is a student’s handwritten course notes, transcribed from scans. The primary lecture and textbook are not in the library.
  • Some statements in the notes look like slips and are not repeated above. Page 48 says 75% of data lie within ±2 and 89% within ±3 standard deviations; these match the Chebyshev lower bounds and not the normal-distribution figures (about 95% and 99.7%), and the notes do not say which applies. The worked example on page 92 compares t = 2.80 with 1.984 for n = 500, but 1.984 is the value near df = 100, and df = 499 gives about 1.965; the conclusion does not change. The central limit theorem line on page 80 reads “S = σ/√N [as written]”, and the proportion test on page 89 writes Hₐ: π ≤ 0.03 where a strict inequality is meant.
  • Pages 54 to 57, 63 to 66, 68 to 73 and 78 are missing from the scan. The probability, Bayes, distribution and binomial sections (pages 53 to 77) are in Probability, Conditional Probability and Bayes’ Rule and Random Variables and Probability Distributions.
  • The formula sheet embedded in the source note (a PDF) was not read into this note, so formulas here come from the handwritten pages only.
  • The course is frequentist and basic. It does not cover effect sizes, multiple testing, regression inference, mixed models, clustering or simulation-based power.

Open

  • Which model family handles repeated rounds nested in players, tables and timelines, and who can check it?
  • What primary outcome is binary, ordinal or continuous, and which formula family follows?
  • How should power be computed for the planned comparisons (by simulation, with which assumed effect)?
