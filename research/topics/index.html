Agent Setup, Rules, Gameplay Structure and Platform

Relevant design patterns

  • Upstream-downstream asymmetry as the core mechanic: WAG, River Basin Game, Lingmuteychu. Water as a token that moves physically.
  • Role swapping to build perspective: River Basin Game (head-enders and tail-enders), Lingmuteychu (third session).
  • Individual then collective phase: River Basin Game moves from private search to collective sharing; Lingmuteychu moves from intra- to inter-village. This mirrors a competitive-to-cooperative (coopetition) sequence.
  • Model-coupled vs model-free: SimBasin, Murray-Darling and Virtual River are coupled to real models; POMCA uses an expert matrix; WAG, River Basin Game and Facing Floods are analogue.
  • Institutional outputs: only the River Basin Game (draft agreements) and Lingmuteychu (a signed committee) report institutions created after play; POMCA feeds an official plan.

Game-by-game extraction

1. Wat-A-Game (WAG)

  • Nature. A toolkit, not one game. It supplies generic “bricks”, guidelines, training and online support so managers, technicians or teachers can build their own water RPGs (Abrami et al. 2012). Invented by Nils Ferrand in 2009. It is part of the CoOPLAGE family from UMR G-EAU (Montpellier). More than 100 games have been built since 2008 (Balaton Group); your table says 150+, which may be a newer count.
  • Space. Land-plot cards connected by hydrological elements (river cards).
  • Agents. Roles chosen by the designers (farmers, decision-makers, citizens, fishers, etc.). Each role carries:
    • objectives (general goals or specific indicators);
    • initial assets (budget, parcels, water access, activities);
    • rules of action (allowed activities, interactions, indicator dynamics).
  • Resources (colour-coded balls/tokens).
    • Blue: mobile, flow in the river, captured by activities as they pass (water).
    • Green: located on plots, renewed between rounds by fixed rules (biodiversity).
    • Yellow: held by players, spendable or tradeable (money).
  • Rules and gameplay. Players place activity cards on their plots. Water tokens are released upstream and captured in sequence downstream, so upstream-downstream asymmetry is built in. The model is designed while it is played.
  • Design process. Framing; system cartography (hydrology, users, activity zones); defining roles, events and monitoring; producing cards and tokens; session with animation and debriefing.
  • Implementation levels. Includes Self-WAG (a blank kit and booklet for building your own catchment game). Known variants: Wet-WAG (wetlands), WAG/Reuse (water reuse with agriculture, industry and urban roles), Waste-WAG (sanitation, Senegal), L’eau en Têt (4 games on the Têt river, France).
  • Platform. Non-computerised (cards, pebbles, marbles). A NetLogo version, ana-wag, lets players play it as a network game or replace them with computer agents (Bonté et al. 2019).
  • Gaps. Turn sequence and scoring are case-specific and in member-only manuals.

2. Concert’eau (the “WAG in contrats de rivière” row)

  • Source. Richard-Ferroudji (2008), thesis surveying three basins (Gardons, Lentilla, Orb), with a game experiment among its methods. The Irstea record describes the game as Concert’eau, used in the Lentilla and Llech case.
  • Purpose. Players move between pragmatic regimes and between different water-management “orders of worth” (Boltanski-Thévenot) to reach compromises.
  • Agents, rules, rounds, platform. n.r. in accessible sources. The full thesis PDF is behind HAL’s bot protection; open it in a browser for chapter detail.

3. River Basin Game (Mkoji, Tanzania)

  • Board. Wooden board about 290-300 cm long, raised about 15 cm at the top end. A central river runs from upper catchment to downstream wetland. Intakes of varying size feed fields with holes that represent crop water need. Also: school/livestock farm, state farm, tail-enders on drain water. Splits into 4 parts; a three-quarter-size version and options (storage dam, boreholes) exist.
  • Water. Glass marbles released at the top. Few marbles = dry year; many = wet year.
  • Agents. About 30-35 water users from upstream, midstream and tail-end; also domestic users, pastoralists, fisherfolk and some leaders. Rain-fed farmers are excluded. Observers (irrigation officers, basin office) bring the room to 50-60. Staff: facilitator, technical assistants, note-takers, video.
  • Rules. Sticks are weirs. Thin sticks = traditional intakes (leaky). Large sticks = modern intakes (block most flow). A plot holding the right number of marbles is adequately irrigated. Golden rules: rehearse all phases; go slowly; clear marbles after each round; allow free discussion before each round; summarise each phase; never skip stages; stress that the board is a model, not a map.
  • Structure (Day 1, about 3-4 h).
    1. Introduction and demonstration (no intakes, many intakes, wet year, dry year; traditional vs modern intakes).
    2. Individual search for water (usually ends in upstream over-abstraction).
    3. Search for livelihoods (renting land, labour; optional paper money). Head-enders and tail-enders swap places.
    4. Collective sharing: weirs are adjusted over several rounds to share by need.
    5. Group discussion (about 1.5 h), reporting and vote.
  • Day 2. Technical focus (water saving) or institutional/legal focus (rights, fees). Brainstorm, vote on priorities, draft agreements naming supporting institutions.
  • Scoring. None. Outcomes are captured through discussion, notes, video and evaluation forms.
  • Cost. Example budget about US$500 for 50 people.
  • Limits. Max about 35 active players; unsuited to piped networks, groundwater or water quality; facilitator-dependent; needs follow-up.

4. Murray-Darling Basin Game (Goulburn-Broken scoping case)

  • Status. Conceptual and in development in 2011. Adapted from the University of Virginia Bay Game.
  • Agents.
    • 5 government authority players (policy varies by scenario).
    • 6 consumptive-user types; 4 described: perennial irrigators (need high security), annual irrigators (sell, hold or substitute water), district farmers without permanent rights (buy opportunistically), out-of-catchment irrigators.
    • Environmental water holder (waters wetlands, may trade within policy).
    • 2 guardians (advocate for environment and communities; do not trade).
  • Rules. Buy, sell or hold water; decide on cropping; release environmental flows; set or follow policy. A causal loop diagram briefs players.
  • Time. Short steps (weeks) for trading and releases, nested in 5-10 year regulatory decisions. Exact step n.r.
  • Platform. Stocks-and-flows simulation (water, money, natural capital). Hydrology from the REALM-based Goulburn System Model; farm functions from ABARES data. Engine software n.r.
  • Indicators. River health (flow duration, height, season), farm income, urban supply security, money and natural-capital stocks.

5. SimBasin (Magdalena-Cauca, Colombia)

  • Agents. Groups of decision-makers from several sectors, scientists and non-experts. Specific roles n.r. in the abstract.
  • Gameplay. Groups steer a fictional basin, resembling the real one, through 30 years of development decisions. Impacts on agriculture, hydropower, flood risk and environment.
  • Indicators. 7, to be kept above critical thresholds. Each shows the realised value and a band of possible values, so players can tell good management from luck.
  • Platform. Game layer over a WEAP model adapted from a basin-wide model; free online (simbasin.hilab.nl). Developed by UNESCO-IHE with The Nature Conservancy Colombia.
  • Gaps. Session length and round granularity n.r. in accessible sources; the full EMS paper has them.

6. POMCA Campoalegre serious game (Colombia)

  • Purpose. Embedded in the formulation phase of the official watershed plan (POMCA): 5 strategic areas, 14 programmes, 25 projects.
  • Agents. Groups of up to 10 drawn from sectors (coffee federation, water utilities, smallholders, NGOs, academics, environmental authority, planning bodies). One moderator per group.
  • Rules and phases.
    1. Briefing; random allocation to groups; interface training.
    2. Group assigns consensus weights (0-100, summing to 100%) to 11 performance components. Weights are then locked.
    3. For each of 4 challenges (rainy season with floods and landslides; tourism/agriculture boom with weak monitoring; dry season with fires and shortages; average season), the group picks an “action package”. A package cannot be reused.
    4. Written justification, then simulation.
    5. Review of maps, bar charts and heatmaps; plenary feedback.
  • Scoring. Sustainability Points per component and sub-basin, propagating downstream, from an expert Leopold matrix against a baseline. No budget mechanic.
  • Platform. R 4.1.0 with Shiny, online; offline via RStudio. Saves choices for replay.
  • Sessions. Two online workshops, June 2021 (COVID-19), plus survey.
  • Limits. Expert judgment rather than a validated hydrological model; needs a computer and prior basin knowledge.

7. Facing Floods (TU Delft)

  • Setting. Bendegom, an idealised Dutch town on the fictional Tulla river.
  • Agents. 5 stakeholders, each with goals and a budget: Rijkswaterstaat (lower flood levels rather than higher dikes), Municipality (housing), navigation sector, farmers (land), nature conservationists. A consultant reviews proposals and places agreed measures. A facilitator runs intro and debrief. The guide covers 7 or more players.
  • Rules. Proposals go to the consultant, then negotiation. Farmer and conservation land cannot change without the owner’s consent; the unowned green area is open. Unmet news-event requirements trigger fines.
  • Structure. 2-3 rounds, each followed by a news event (population surge; food self-sufficiency law; shipping demand; nature-based protection law), drawn or chosen. Final debrief.
  • Scoring. 5 criteria (flood safety, urban life, nature, agricultural yield, navigability), each -10 to +10, on a dashboard.
  • Platform. Printable board (A4/A3), CC BY-NC-SA 4.0.

8. Virtual River Game (Netherlands)

  • Agents. Domain experts (hydraulic engineers, flood-safety specialists) and non-experts (residents, farmers). Experts played roles unlike their real jobs.
  • Rules. Players swap modular pieces to change a tile’s elevation, land use or both, freely designing Dutch-style interventions. Each tested design updates scores.
  • Scoring. Flood safety, biodiversity, costs.
  • Platform. Hybrid interface with bidirectional coupling: a physical hexagonal board of 143 tiles is converted into a digital elevation model and land-use map; hydrodynamic, ecological and cost models run; outputs are projected on the board and shown on a monitor.
  • Evaluation. Five sessions with experts and non-experts (den Haan et al. 2020).

9. Lords of the Valley (hypothetical valley)

  • Agents. 15-25 participants:
    • Farmers: choose production per plot (grain, livestock, orchards, fish ponds, forestry); can buy land.
    • Local Government: controls free plots, sets taxes, gives subsidies.
    • Water Board: builds and maintains dykes and a water-steering system.
    • Bank: grants and collects loans.
    • Environmental NGO: knows biodiversity effects; can complain to Central Government (played by the moderator).
  • Environment. Short river reach with yearly averages; floods and droughts hit production differently by type; intensification raises pest losses.
  • Rules. 7 rounds; free decisions; facilitator restricts talk (no more than three roles at once); 2 community meetings with self-set procedures.
  • Outcomes. Accumulated profit and biodiversity; ecosystem damage feeds back on profit.
  • Platform. Online on tablets and computers. 4-5 h play plus 1-2 h debrief.

10. Get the Grade (WWF and UMCES)

  • Agents. Each player is a stakeholder living in a sub-basin. Room = basin; table = sub-basin. Audiences: policy-makers, conservationists, companies, communities.
  • Gameplay. Teams build a Basin Health Report Card for a fictitious basin, then take governance decisions and negotiate partnerships to raise the grade. Grades rest on six values: ecological; water quality and quantity; social and cultural; health and nutrition; management and governance; economic.
  • Platform. Table game with map illustration and dice; scales from small meetings to conferences. Launched in 2016 (Stockholm, Honolulu, New Delhi).
  • Gaps. Dice mechanics, rounds and scoring n.r. publicly; WWF supplies kits on request.

11. ComMod Lingmuteychu (Bhutan)

  • Context. Rain-fed stream from a spring at 2400 m; 12 canals irrigate about 180 ha of terraces; a long-running conflict over water at rice transplanting.
  • Cycle 1 (2003), “2 villages”. Upper and lower village farmers. 2D board of land by farm type. Players handle crops, water cards, money and labour. Three sessions: intra-village, inter-village, swapped roles. A new rule was agreed but later rejected by the upper village.
  • Cycle 2 (2005), “7 villages”. All seven villages. Highly abstract board; only water is manipulated. Same three-session design. Result: a Watershed Management Committee, signed by all; bylaws late 2005; formally established December 2005.
  • Platform. Board RPG, translated into a CORMAS multi-agent model for lab scenario runs.
  • Gaps. Player counts, round numbers and MAS parameters n.r.
