Summary

Future generations cannot reciprocate, cannot veto and cannot retaliate, so cooperation with them is not enforceable by ordinary means. The sources test levers in intergenerational goods games: binding votes, veto power for later generations, a personal stake in the future, nudges and an imagined future generation. A recurring result is that coordination among contemporaries is often the binding constraint, not concern for the future. None of the abstracts tests whether a cooperative cohort passes a cooperative norm on to its successors, which is the gap Option C2 targets.

Claims

  1. Structure of the problem. In the Intergenerational Goods Game, a line of successive groups can extract a resource to exhaustion or leave something for the next group; “future generations cannot reciprocate actions made today” (Hauser et al. (2014)).
  2. Individual extraction fails. When extraction is decided individually the resource is almost always destroyed, driven by a minority who extract far more than is sustainable. In the unregulated treatment 68% of individuals cooperated, yet only 4 of 18 games continuing to a second generation kept their pool (Hauser et al. (2014)).
  3. Binding democracy works. With a median vote binding for all, all 20 pools were sustained across generations. Voting helps for two reasons: a majority of cooperators can restrain defectors, and it reassures conditional cooperators that their effort is not futile. It promotes sustainability only if binding (Hauser et al. (2014)).
  4. Conflict inside a generation. With one decision maker per generation, resources are almost always preserved. With three contemporaries deciding simultaneously without communication, conservation collapses. The explanation is intergenerational altruism combined with beliefs about others’ restraint: pessimistic expectations erode altruistic motives (Bayle et al. (2026)).
  5. Veto for later generations. In an online experiment (682 participants) early generations’ consumption does not depend on how many generations follow. A veto for later generations gives fairer but less efficient use, and vetoes are used more carefully when generations that do not yet have access are also affected (Farjam and Wolf (2021)).
  6. Stake and perceived group size. Having a stake in the future (50% chance of passing the resource on to oneself) raises cooperation, except when people-chatting sounds are played. The authors suggest the sounds trigger a perception of a large group, which lowers the chance that one’s own descendants benefit (Chang et al. (2021)).
  7. Short-term versus long-term costs and nudges. Players reach enough cooperation to avoid harm to their own generation but contribute more to cheaper short-term than to costlier long-term measures. Setting the default to the intergenerational optimum raises long-term contributions, and a commitment option raises them further. Nudges alone may not be enough (Böhm et al. (2020); your note: “good paper to inspire”).
  8. Imaginary future generation. When some current players act as an imaginary future generation negotiating with present players, 60% of participants chose the sustainable option versus 28% when the imaginary generation was not salient (Kamijo et al. (2017)).

Inferences

  1. The generational timeline design extends the Intergenerational Goods Game. The design has overlapping tenures and full-state bequest to an unknown successor, as in Hauser et al. (2014), but with persistent timelines, six players per seat set and 24 weekly sessions, so that successors become real participants and not simulated ones.
  2. The binding constraint may be the cohort, not the future. If Bayle et al. (2026) holds, then within-cohort coordination, communication and group size need to be controlled or manipulated alongside the future-generation cue. Otherwise a failure to cooperate with the future would be misread as shortsightedness.
  3. Voice and nudge are competing mechanisms. Farjam and Wolf (2021) test voice (veto), Böhm et al. (2020) test nudges (default and commitment), and Kamijo et al. (2017) test salience. The Open Call chain’s “voice > nudge” hypothesis (H2) compares exactly these two families, and the sources do not compare them in one design.
  4. Perceived group size is a manipulable variable. Chang et al. (2021) show a perceived-density effect from sounds alone. In a large-group design, what the interface shows about the number of cohorts and participants is a design lever and a possible confound.
  5. Inheritability is open. Among these eight abstracts, none compares successors who inherit a healthy stock with those who inherit a depleted one while holding the stock mechanics constant. The gap claim in the proposals rests on this; it should be checked against the full texts.

Limits

  • Hauser et al. (2014) has no abstract in the source note; claims 1 to 3 come from the first page of the PDF.
  • Most designs run a handful of generations. Whether effects last over 24 sessions is untested here.
  • Bayle et al. (2026) and Farjam and Wolf (2021) are recent or preprint-like; check their publication status before citing.
  • Samples and settings differ (lab, lab in the field, online), so effect sizes are not comparable across the sources.

Open

  • Does a successor inheriting a healthy stock cooperate more, beyond what the stock mechanically forces (Open Call H3)?
  • Does a veto or voice mechanism outperform an equivalent private nudge?
  • How is the final-tenure test (a departing player’s last session) best separated from instrumental cooperation?
  • Which hand-over design keeps immersion (see Natural Funativity and the Evolution of Play)?
