Summary

“Contract” is read in two senses. The first is the Italian Contratto di Fiume (CdF), a voluntary negotiated agreement (how it works: Contratti di Fiume). The second is the economic sense: a payment or compensation rule between upstream providers and downstream beneficiaries (PES, trust contracts). Games exist for both, and Camaiore needs both. The transferable part of large-basin games is their mechanism (vertical asymmetry, compensation, negotiation of rules), not their scale.

Games are sorted into three families by what they produce:

  • (A) controlled behavioural experiments on vertical (upstream/downstream) cooperation: they isolate the core dilemma, where upstream actors provide and downstream actors benefit,
  • (B) contract and payment design experiments: they test which contract terms work before they are written into an agreement,
  • (C) participatory role-playing and serious games for co-designing agreements: they produce agreements and rules, not just data.

All source notes behind this note are still seed (read from abstracts and catalogue records only), so the claims below are provisional.

Claims

  1. No evidence of a signed or active Contratto di Fiume Camaiore. This needs checking with the Comune, the Consorzio Toscana Nord, and the Regione’s CdF registry.

Family A: behavioural experiments on vertical cooperation

  1. Irrigation Game (A1). In field experiments in Colombia and Kenya, participants rarely contributed nothing; face-to-face communication was the most effective way to raise cooperation, working considerably better in Colombia than in Kenya; asymmetric appropriation reduced cooperation, with the size of the social loss varying by site (Cárdenas et al. (2011), abstract).
  2. Water Trust Game (A1). With 639 watershed inhabitants, the Water Trust Game (a Berg et al. trust game framed as water versus compensation flows, real upstream or downstream location revealed) and the Irrigation Game showed that location shapes provision and distribution, that reciprocity and trust drive upstream–downstream cooperation, and that downstream distrust of upstream players may limit it (Cárdenas et al. (2015), abstract).
  3. Enforcement and crowding out (A2). In an investment game with upstream and downstream players in Nyanza, Kenya, downstream compensation was enforced for one round. When enforcement was removed in round two, upstream first movers responded in a way consistent with crowding out of social preferences (Jack (2009), abstract).
  4. Non-binding contracts in a basin (A3). The Syr Darya modelled as a multi-round, three-player trust game with non-binding contracts and payoffs estimated from real data: cooperation was hard to achieve in the laboratory although basin-wide efficiency requires it (Abbink et al. (2010), abstract).

Family B: contract and payment design experiments

  1. Agglomeration bonus, field evidence (B1). In a framed field experiment with Chinese farmers, subjects tended to bid lower in anticipation of the bonus when neighbours were also successful; evidence that the bonus induces contiguous conservation was mixed (Liu et al. (2019), abstract).
  2. Agglomeration bonus versus joint bidding (B1). With 432 farmers in four treatments, adding joint bidding lowered environmental performance and cost-effectiveness; the bonus achieved higher environmental performance than joint bidding at similar cost-effectiveness (Liu et al. (2024), abstract).
  3. Individual versus collective contracts (B2). A registered lab-in-the-field design with 200 smallholder cattle farmers in South Africa plays five contracts (observable input versus measured output, with and without a production shock, with and without reward for unobserved contributions) alone and in groups of about five. No results are reported (Malagutti (2026), registry page).

Family C: participatory role-playing and serious games

  1. Wat-A-Game. A generic platform, developed by Cemagref/INRAE and CIRAD, for role-playing games on integrated water management at small-catchment scale, using marbles as water drops and cards for plots, actions and roles (Ferrand et al. (2009), catalogue records).
  2. River Basin Game. In the Mkoji sub-catchment (Tanzania), players learned that being at the top of the river has advantages while tail-end systems run short, and that community action beat individual strategies for equitable allocation (Rajabu (2007), catalogue description).
  3. Simulation games for large basins. The Murray-Darling Basin Game concerns reflexivity about model building and game play (Moore et al. (2011)); a Magdalena-Cauca game lets players steer a fictional basin for 30 years, simulated in WEAP, to open dialogue between policy makers and scientists (Craven et al. (2017)).
  4. Games inside a statutory plan. A serious game supported the POMCA (watershed management plan) of the Campoalegre basin, which has five strategic focus areas, 14 programmes and 25 projects; it targets participants’ lack of awareness of the watershed’s state (Gonzalez et al. (2024), catalogue records).
  5. Agreement and institution. A role-playing game turned into a multi-agent model, then a second model for institution building, is reported to have produced a concrete agreement and an institution for collective watershed management in Bhutan (Gurung et al. (2006), abstract).

Inferences

  1. The Water Trust Game is the closest model of a compensation clause. Coastal and tourism actors paying hill landowners for retention and maintenance has the same structure as a trust game framed as water versus compensation (claim 3). Its finding that downstream distrust limits cooperation suggests the Camaiore design should vary what downstream players can observe about upstream effort.
  2. Binding or “soft” clause is an empirical question. Claim 4 tests whether enforcement on one side changes voluntary reciprocity on the other. Whether that carries over to a CdF is uncertain: Jack’s enforcement falls on downstream payers and the crowding-out is seen in upstream players. It links to Incentives, Payment Schemes and Crowding Out.
  3. The Syr Darya design is portable to a non-binding CdF. A CdF is voluntary and non-binding, like the contracts in claim 5. The scale differs (three states against many private users), so only the contract mechanism transfers, not the calibration.
  4. Local institutions can be a treatment. A design that adds an existing local institution (the Consorzio di Bonifica, volunteer associations) to a compensation game would test whether it strengthens the scheme. The A4 entry in the working list (an Ethiopian lab-in-the-field on downstream compensation and a customary system) could not be found in the registry, so this rests on our own idea only (see Open).
  5. Contiguity makes the auction format relevant. Flood retention along a corridor only works if plots are contiguous, so an agglomeration-bonus or joint-bidding auction is a candidate way to allocate “space for the river” contracts among hill and plain landowners. Claim 7 warns that joint bidding did worse than the bonus in the China field experiment.
  6. Pay individuals or a collective is a design variable. Claim 8 offers a template for comparing individual landowners with a consortium or association as the paid agent. It connects to the finding in Zhang et al. (2022) that individual incentivised payments lowered group harvest relative to a community flat payment.
  7. Family C produces rules, Families A and B produce estimates. The role-playing games (claims 9 to 13) are the route to a participatory phase of a “Towards a CdF” process; the controlled games are the route to confirmatory evidence. This is the dual register of Games as Interventional and Participatory Research Tools. The evidence that role-playing games change real outcomes rests on case reports (claims 10 and 13) and is weak.
  8. Borrow mechanisms, not scale. Large-basin games model many jurisdictions; Camaiore’s vertical conflict is between users within one municipality (table below). Vertical asymmetry, compensation and rule negotiation transfer.

Camaiore context (evidence and what it implies for game design):

Feature Evidence Implication for game design
Basin ≈ municipality Observations to the Serchio flood plan note that the Camaiore river basin coincides with the territory of the municipality of the same name (MASE VA doc 139027) Few jurisdictions. So the vertical conflict is between users (hill farmers and landowners, the plain, the coast and tourism), not between municipalities
Flood risk is the dominant issue Expansion basins (casse di espansione) are still incomplete decades after the 1996 Versilia flood (La Nazione); the Region is running the adjustment to the 200-year flow and the Magazzeno expansion basin (Regione Toscana) The “resource” in the game is flood-risk reduction and retention, not water allocation. Upstream provision (land maintenance, retention, space for the river) benefits the downstream plain and coast
Coastal water quality Lido di Camaiore ranked among the worst sampled sites in Legambiente’s Goletta Verde (La Nazione) A second upstream to downstream externality: pollution reaching the beach economy. This is a natural PES framing
Contested “space for the river” Critics argue the safety project widened the channel instead of giving the river more room in harmony with nature (MASE VA doc 139027) A game can make the trade-off between hard works and nature-based retention visible to stakeholders
Institutional neighbourhood Camaiore sits in the UoM Toscana Nord (Baccatoio, Camaiore, Versilia…) and partly in the Serchio basin (MASE VA doc 137652); Lucca Province ran a pilot “Towards a River Contract for the Serchio” under the MED project WaterInCore (Lazzari, partecipa.gov.it); Tuscany’s 2019 to 2021 call funded a Massaciuccoli lake contract (Massarosa with Vecchiano) (gonews) Existing regional CdF funding lines and neighbouring precedents. A game can serve as the participatory phase of a funded “Towards a CdF” process
Volunteer maintenance already exists The Consorzio di Bonifica Toscana Nord’s “gentle hydraulic maintenance” lab includes the Camaiore river (MeteoWeb) A real-world behavioural outcome you can measure after the game (participation in maintenance), as Bartels et al. did with dam maintenance

Limits

  • Thin reading. All thirteen new source notes are seed. Claims 2 to 13 come from abstracts, registry pages and catalogue records, not from the full papers. Sample sizes, effect sizes and payment realism are unchecked.
  • Registry, not results. Malagutti (2026) is a trial registration with no results, entered after the fieldwork dates it lists.
  • Family C evidence is descriptive. Claims 9 to 13 describe tools and case reports. They do not show that a game changed behaviour or produced an agreement beyond what the authors report.
  • Our transfers are untested. Inferences 2 and 3 move findings from Kenyan fieldwork and a three-state basin to private users in a short Italian stream.
  • Corrections to the working list. The Syr Darya paper is Abbink, Moller & O’Hara (University of Nottingham working paper 2005, published 2010), not a Bocconi study; IGIER hosts a copy. The “Cardenas et al., Environment and Development Economics” paper is a separate 2011 paper from the Water Trust Game working paper. The Liu et al. (2019) study is a framed field experiment with farmers, which may differ from “real forest landowners”.
  • Meinzen-Dick et al. (2018), World Development 107:40 to 53 (EconPapers): after groundwater games in Andhra Pradesh, significantly more communities adopted water registers and rules than comparison communities. Games alone do not stop depletion, though. (In project files.)
  • Falk et al. (2019), Agricultural Water Management 221:260 to 269 (Rajasthan; in project files).
  • Bartels et al. (2022), JEEM 113:102631 (in project files): a two-phase design. Rounds 1 to 5 form a controlled, anonymous, no-communication experiment; rounds 6 to 10 are a context-rich learning phase. Payment form had little effect in-game. Some evidence suggests real-world change, and the authors warn that players may use the game as a “playground” rather than revealing real behaviour.

Open

  • Scale mismatch: big-basin games (Murray-Darling, Syr Darya, Magdalena) model many jurisdictions and allocation trading. Camaiore is a short, steep, flood-dominated Apuan stream. Borrow mechanisms, not calibration.
  • External validity of framed games is contested (Bartels et al. 2022). Plan an observable real-world outcome from the start.
  • Italian gap: I found critical scholarship on Italian river contracts (participation, politicisation), e.g. Di Quarto & Venturini 2025, Shima, Brusarosco 2025, Belgeo, and Altamore & De Leo 2023. I found no published experimental game used inside an Italian CdF. This looks like a genuine research gap and a possible contribution of the thesis.
  • Which family first? Which of A, B and C does the Camaiore proposal rest on, and how are they sequenced?
  • Unverified list items (no source note yet): A4, an AEA RCT Registry entry AEARCTR-0015283 on downstream compensation and the Yewuha Abat customary system in Ethiopia (2025): neither the ID nor the name turned up in searches. The French river-contract thesis (HAL tel-02590578) on Wat-A-Game in the Gardons, Lentilla and Orb: the HAL page could not be opened. Games with no citable publication found so far: Facing Floods (TU Delft), Virtual River, Lords of the Valley (IIASA), Get the Grade (WWF).
  • Project-file sources without a source note: Ostrom (2000) Crowding Out Citizenship (Scandinavian Political Studies 23(1):3–16), Meinzen-Dick et al. (2018), Falk et al. (2019) and Bartels et al. (2022) are cited in this note but have no source note and are not in the library folder.
  • To verify: the Camaiore CdF status; Camaiore basin area and land-use data; whether WAG licensing (CC-BY-NC-SA, signed user charter) fits your project.
