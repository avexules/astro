Summary

Individuals differ in how much they care about others’ outcomes, and several instruments try to capture this: social value orientation measures, a two-scale cooperative and competitive personality scale, and conditional-cooperation classification in a public goods game. Their predictive value for cooperative behaviour is real but modest, and their link to field behaviour is weak. For the PhD they are candidate covariates and a way to describe who ends up in which seat, not the main outcome.

Claims

  1. Beyond narrow self-interest. People show a wide range of motivations when choosing among interdependent outcomes. Social value orientation (SVO) measures the concern for others, and a new measure with good psychometric properties is introduced (Murphy et al. (2011)).
  2. Modest predictive power. SVO and cooperation in social dilemmas relate at r = .30 in a meta-analysis of 82 studies, lower when participants are paid (Balliet et al. (2009)).
  3. A second construct. The Cooperative and Competitive Personality Scale predicts cooperative behaviour in a public goods dilemma and in a commons dilemma when SVO is controlled, so it supplements SVO (Lu et al. (2013)).
  4. Conditional cooperation. Students classified as conditional cooperators in an online public goods game adjust contributions to others in a field setting, and low unconditional contributors free ride more in the field (Reindl et al. (2019)).
  5. Weak field validity. Social preference games explain social behaviour in the field and from the past poorly (Galizzi and Navarro-Martinez (2019)).
  6. Data quality of the measurement setting. Online samples differ in attention, comprehension, honesty and reliability by platform (Peer et al. (2021)).

Inferences

  1. Use scales as covariates and for seat composition, not as outcomes. Given claims 2 and 5, preference measures describe participants and help address the composition-versus-state confound in the generational design; they will not carry the main results.
  2. Keep cooperativeness and competitiveness separate. If Lu et al. (2013) holds, one SVO score cannot summarise both. See Coopetition as Two Independent Dimensions.
  3. Repeated measurement could test stability. A 24-week design allows re-measuring preferences. Whether they change after a tenure in a given regime is a possible secondary question; it is our idea, not tested in these sources.
  4. Pay and attention checks affect the instruments. Claims 2 and 6 imply that payment for the measure and comprehension checks need deciding up front.

Limits

  • Thin set: six sources, with abstracts only.
  • The Murphy note describes the measure but not its norming data or limits.
  • Whether any of these instruments work in an online large-group setting with a few minutes of survey time is not shown.

Open

  • Which instrument is the best compromise between length and validity?
  • Does a pre-game SVO measure change behaviour in the game (priming)?
  • How should composition effects be modelled in the analysis (random effects by seat, timeline and player)?
