Summary

Purpose. This course gives you only the matrix basics you need to follow your syllabus. Each section ends with a ➜ Where you’ll use this note that links it to a topic in your course. Your syllabus follows Gilbert Strang’s approach closely (the “four fundamental subspaces”, $PA=LU$, $A=QR$, the SVD). For that reason, Strang’s book and MIT 18.06 lectures are the best companion material (see References).

How to read it. Sections 1 to 6 are the core; read them first and do the exercises. Sections 7 to 12 are short previews of ideas your course will develop properly. Section 13 is the small amount of “matrix calculus” you need for least squares.

0. Notation cheat sheet

Symbol Meaning
$a, \lambda, c$ scalars (ordinary numbers)
$\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{b}$ (or just $x, v, b$) vectors, written as columns by default
$A, B, Q, L, U$ matrices
$a_{ij}$ entry of $A$ in row $i$, column $j$
$A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ $A$ has $m$ rows and $n$ columns, real entries
$A^T$ transpose (rows become columns)
$I$ (or $I_n$) identity matrix
$A^{-1}$ inverse of $A$
$\det A$, $\operatorname{tr} A$ determinant, trace (sum of diagonal entries)
$\lVert x\rVert$ length (norm) of $x$
$x^T y$ dot product of $x$ and $y$
$\operatorname{rank} A$ number of independent columns (= number of pivots)
$C(A), N(A)$ column space, null space

Rule of thumb on sizes. Always write the size under a matrix when you are unsure. Most beginner errors are size errors.

1. Vectors

A vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is an ordered list of $n$ numbers, written as a column: $$ v=\begin{bmatrix}1\3\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^2,\qquad w=\begin{bmatrix}2\-1\0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^3 . $$ Think of it as an arrow from the origin to a point.

Two basic operations (they are done entry by entry):

  • Addition: $\begin{bmatrix}1\3\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}2\4\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}3\7\end{bmatrix}$
  • Scalar multiplication: $5\begin{bmatrix}1\3\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}5\15\end{bmatrix}$

Linear combination. Combine both operations: $c,v + d,w$. This is the central operation of all linear algebra. Every later concept (span, column space, solving $Ax=b$) is a question about linear combinations.

Expanded: how to build and read a linear combination.

Recipe. A linear combination of vectors $v_1,\dots,v_k$ is any vector of the form $$ c_1v_1+c_2v_2+\dots+c_kv_k , $$ where the numbers $c_1,\dots,c_k$ are called the weights (or coefficients). You do two things only: scale each vector by its own weight, then add the results entry by entry. All vectors must have the same length, otherwise the sum is undefined.

Worked example. Take $v=\begin{bmatrix}1\3\end{bmatrix}$ and $w=\begin{bmatrix}2\4\end{bmatrix}$, with weights $c=2$ and $d=-1$: $$ 2v-w = 2\begin{bmatrix}1\3\end{bmatrix}+(-1)\begin{bmatrix}2\4\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}2\6\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}-2\-4\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}0\2\end{bmatrix}. $$ Changing the weights gives a different vector from the same two ingredients: $c=1,\ d=1$ gives $\begin{bmatrix}3\7\end{bmatrix}$, $c=0,\ d=0$ gives the zero vector, $c=1,\ d=0$ gives $v$ itself. So $v$, $w$ and $0$ are all special cases of one formula.

Geometric picture. Draw $v$ and $w$ as arrows from the origin. The weight $c$ stretches or flips $v$ along its own line, the weight $d$ does the same to $w$, and the sum is the diagonal of the parallelogram built from the two stretched arrows (place the tail of one at the tip of the other). Think of $v$ and $w$ as two directions you may walk in, and of $(c,d)$ as how far you walk in each. The combination is where you end up.

The question linear algebra keeps asking: which vectors can I reach?

  • Forward direction. Given weights, compute the vector. This is just arithmetic, as above.
  • Backward direction. Given a target $b$, find weights that produce it. Example: can we reach $b=\begin{bmatrix}4\10\end{bmatrix}$ with the same $v,w$? We need $c,v+d,w=b$, that is $$ c+2d=4,\qquad 3c+4d=10 . $$ The first equation gives $c=4-2d$. Substituting into the second: $12-6d+4d=10$, so $d=1$ and $c=2$. Check: $2v+w=\begin{bmatrix}2\6\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}2\4\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}4\10\end{bmatrix}$. Yes.

The backward direction is exactly a linear system, and the system is $Ax=b$ with the vectors as columns of $A$ and the weights as the unknown $x$: $$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&2\3&4\end{bmatrix}}{A=[,v\ \ w,]} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}c\d\end{bmatrix}}{x} =\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}4\10\end{bmatrix}}_{b}. $$ This is why section 3 says that $Ax$ is a combination of the columns of $A$.

Not every target is reachable. The set of all combinations of some vectors is their span. Two vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$ that point in different directions can reach every point of the plane. But if they point along the same line, for instance $v=\begin{bmatrix}1\2\end{bmatrix}$ and $w=\begin{bmatrix}2\4\end{bmatrix}$ (here $w=2v$), every combination $c,v+d,w=(c+2d),v$ stays on that one line. The target $b=\begin{bmatrix}1\0\end{bmatrix}$ would need $c+2d=1$ and $2c+4d=0$, which contradict each other, so no weights exist. In the same way, two vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$ reach only a plane through the origin, for example $v=\begin{bmatrix}1\0\1\end{bmatrix}$ and $w=\begin{bmatrix}0\1\1\end{bmatrix}$ give $\begin{bmatrix}c\d\c+d\end{bmatrix}$, the plane $z=x+y$. Sections 5 and 8 make “can I reach $b$?” precise through column space, span and independence.

Linear combinations of matrices. The same recipe works for matrices of the same size, entry by entry. With $A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\0&1\end{bmatrix}$ and $B=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\4&1\end{bmatrix}$: $$ 2A-3B=\begin{bmatrix}2&4\0&2\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}3&0\12&3\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-1&4\-12&-1\end{bmatrix}. $$ This is just addition and scalar multiplication again, with matrices playing the role of vectors. Do not confuse it with the matrix product $AB$ (section 4), which is a different operation. The combination you will use most is the other one: a matrix times a vector, $Ax$, which is a combination of the columns of $A$.

Three habits.

  1. Write the weights next to each vector before adding: $c_1,(\ldots)+c_2,(\ldots)$. Most slips happen when the scaling and the adding are done in one step.
  2. Check sizes: all vectors (or matrices) in the combination must have the same shape.
  3. Ask both questions: “what do I get for these weights?” and “which weights give this target?” The second one is solving $Ax=b$.

Dot product (also called inner product): $$ x^T y = x_1y_1 + x_2y_2 + \dots + x_ny_n . $$ Example: $(1,2,3)\cdot(4,0,-1) = 4+0-3 = 1$.

Length (Euclidean norm): $\lVert x\rVert = \sqrt{x^T x}$. Example: $\lVert(3,4)\rVert = 5$.

Angle: $\cos\theta = \dfrac{x^T y}{\lVert x\rVert,\lVert y\rVert}$.

Orthogonal (perpendicular): $x^T y = 0$. Example: $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$ are orthogonal.

Unit vector: length 1. Normalise any nonzero $x$ with $x/\lVert x\rVert$.

➜ Where you’ll use this: everywhere. Dot products and orthogonality drive least squares, $QR$, the spectral theorem and the SVD.

2. Matrices: shapes and special types

A matrix is a rectangular table of numbers. An $m\times n$ matrix has $m$ rows and $n$ columns: $$ A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\3&4\5&6\end{bmatrix}\quad (3\times 2). $$

Two ways to see a matrix (keep both in your head):

  1. As a stack of rows (each row is a vector, used for dot products).
  2. As a row of columns $A = [,a_1\ \ a_2\ \cdots\ a_n,]$ (each column is a vector, used for combinations).

Transpose. $(A^T){ij} = a{ji}$. Rows become columns: $$ \begin{bmatrix}1&2\3&4\5&6\end{bmatrix}^T=\begin{bmatrix}1&3&5\2&4&6\end{bmatrix}. $$ Rules: $(A^T)^T=A$, $(A+B)^T=A^T+B^T$, and $(AB)^T = B^TA^T$ (the order reverses).

Special matrices you will meet constantly:

Name Definition Example
Square $m=n$
Identity $I$ 1 on the diagonal, 0 elsewhere; $IA=AI=A$ $\begin{bmatrix}1&0\0&1\end{bmatrix}$
Diagonal nonzero only on the diagonal $\begin{bmatrix}3&0\0&-2\end{bmatrix}$
Upper triangular $U$ zeros below the diagonal $\begin{bmatrix}2&1\0&5\end{bmatrix}$
Lower triangular $L$ zeros above the diagonal $\begin{bmatrix}1&0\4&1\end{bmatrix}$
Symmetric $A^T=A$ $\begin{bmatrix}2&1\1&2\end{bmatrix}$
Permutation $P$ rows of $I$ reordered; $PA$ reorders the rows of $A$ $\begin{bmatrix}0&1\1&0\end{bmatrix}$
Orthogonal $Q$ columns are orthonormal; $Q^TQ=I$ rotation matrices

➜ Where you’ll use this: $L$, $U$, $P$ in $PA=LU$. Symmetric matrices in the spectral theorem and quadratic forms. Orthogonal $Q$ in $A=QR$ and in the SVD.

3. Matrix times vector (the most important idea)

$Ax$ is defined only when the number of columns of $A$ equals the number of entries of $x$. An $(m\times n)$ matrix times an $(n\times 1)$ vector gives an $(m\times 1)$ vector.

Example. $A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\3&4\end{bmatrix}$, $x=\begin{bmatrix}5\6\end{bmatrix}$.

View 1: by rows (dot products). Each output entry is a row of $A$ dotted with $x$: $$ Ax=\begin{bmatrix}1\cdot5+2\cdot6\3\cdot5+4\cdot6\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}17\39\end{bmatrix}. $$

View 2: by columns (linear combination). $Ax$ is a combination of the columns of $A$, with weights taken from $x$: $$ Ax = 5\begin{bmatrix}1\3\end{bmatrix}+6\begin{bmatrix}2\4\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}17\39\end{bmatrix}. $$

Learn View 2 by heart. It is the key to the whole course:

$Ax$ is a linear combination of the columns of $A$. So $Ax=b$ has a solution exactly when $b$ is a combination of the columns of $A$, i.e. when $b$ lies in the column space $C(A)$.

Matrix as a function. $x\mapsto Ax$ is a linear map: $A(cx+dy)=c,Ax+d,Ay$. Geometrically, a $2\times2$ matrix can rotate, stretch, shear or flatten the plane. The columns of $A$ tell you where the basis vectors $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ land. 3Blue1Brown’s videos show this beautifully (see References).

➜ Where you’ll use this: column space and null space (four subspaces), solvability of $Ax=b$, iterative methods ($x_{k+1}=Mx_k+c$), dynamical systems ($x_{k+1}=Ax_k$).

4. Matrix times matrix

$AB$ is defined when (columns of $A$) = (rows of $B$): $$ \underbrace{A}{m\times n}\ \underbrace{B}{n\times p} = \underbrace{AB}_{m\times p}. $$

Entry rule. $(AB)_{ij}$ = (row $i$ of $A$) · (column $j$ of $B$).

Column rule (often more useful). Column $j$ of $AB$ equals $A$ times column $j$ of $B$.

Row rule. Row $i$ of $AB$ equals (row $i$ of $A$) times $B$, i.e. a combination of the rows of $B$.

Column-times-row rule. $AB = \sum_k (\text{column } k \text{ of } A)(\text{row } k \text{ of } B)$. This is a sum of rank-one matrices, and it is exactly the idea behind the SVD.

Example: order matters. Let $A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\3&4\end{bmatrix}$ and $P=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\1&0\end{bmatrix}$. $$ AP=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\4&3\end{bmatrix}\ (\text{columns swapped}),\qquad PA=\begin{bmatrix}3&4\1&2\end{bmatrix}\ (\text{rows swapped}). $$ So $AB\neq BA$ in general. Multiplying on the left acts on rows. Multiplying on the right acts on columns.

Laws that DO hold:

  • Associative: $(AB)C = A(BC)$
  • Distributive: $A(B+C)=AB+AC$
  • $IA=AI=A$
  • $(AB)^T=B^TA^T$ and $(AB)^{-1}=B^{-1}A^{-1}$ (order reverses)

Laws that do NOT hold (classic traps):

  • $AB = BA$ (false in general)
  • $AB=0 \Rightarrow A=0$ or $B=0$ (false)
  • $AB=AC \Rightarrow B=C$ (false, unless $A$ is invertible)

Powers. $A^2=AA$, $A^k = A\cdots A$. Only square matrices have powers.

Block matrices. You can partition matrices into blocks and multiply block by block, as if the blocks were numbers, provided the sizes fit. Example: $\begin{bmatrix}A&B\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\y\end{bmatrix}=Ax+By$.

➜ Where you’ll use this: factorizations ($LU$, $QR$, $Q\Lambda Q^T$, $U\Sigma V^T$) are all products. Cayley–Hamilton and functions of matrices use powers $A^k$.

5. Linear systems $Ax=b$

A system of $m$ linear equations in $n$ unknowns $$ \begin{aligned} 2x_1 + x_2 + x_3 &= 5\ 4x_1 - 6x_2 \phantom{{}+x_3} &= -2\ -2x_1 + 7x_2 + 2x_3 &= 9 \end{aligned} $$ is a single matrix equation $Ax=b$: $$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}2&1&1\4&-6&0\-2&7&2\end{bmatrix}}{A} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}x_1\x_2\x_3\end{bmatrix}}{x}= \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}5\-2\9\end{bmatrix}}_{b}. $$

Two pictures:

  • Row picture: each equation is a plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$. The solution is where all the planes meet.
  • Column picture: find weights $x_1,x_2,x_3$ so that $x_1(\text{col }1)+x_2(\text{col }2)+x_3(\text{col }3)=b$.

Only three things can happen:

  1. Exactly one solution (the “nice” case; $A$ square and invertible).
  2. No solution (inconsistent; $b$ is not in $C(A)$). Least squares deals with this case.
  3. Infinitely many solutions (there are free variables; $N(A)$ contains nonzero vectors).

It is never “exactly two”. If $x$ and $y$ are both solutions, every point $x + t(y-x)$ on the line through them is also a solution.

Structure of all solutions: $x = x_p + x_n$, where $x_p$ is one particular solution and $x_n$ is any vector with $Ax_n=0$ (the null space).

6. Gaussian elimination, by hand

Goal. Turn $A$ into an upper triangular $U$ using row operations, then solve from the bottom up (back substitution).

The three elementary row operations (none of them changes the solution set):

  1. Subtract a multiple of one row from another row.
  2. Swap two rows.
  3. Multiply a row by a nonzero number.

Work on the augmented matrix $[A\mid b]$.

Worked example (the system from Section 5; this is Strang’s standard example): $$ \left[\begin{array}{rrr|r}2&1&1&5\4&-6&0&-2\-2&7&2&9\end{array}\right] $$

Step 1. The pivot is 2 (top left). Clear the entries below it.

  • Row 2 − 2·Row 1 → $[0,,-8,,-2 \mid -12]$ (multiplier $\ell_{21}=2$)
  • Row 3 − (−1)·Row 1 → $[0,,8,,3 \mid 14]$ (multiplier $\ell_{31}=-1$)

Step 2. The pivot is −8. Clear the entry below it.

  • Row 3 − (−1)·Row 2 → $[0,,0,,1 \mid 2]$ (multiplier $\ell_{32}=-1$)

$$ \left[\begin{array}{rrr|r}2&1&1&5\0&-8&-2&-12\0&0&1&2\end{array}\right] \quad\Rightarrow\quad U=\begin{bmatrix}2&1&1\0&-8&-2\0&0&1\end{bmatrix} $$ Pivots: 2, −8, 1 (all nonzero, so there is a unique solution).

Back substitution:

  • $x_3 = 2$
  • $-8x_2 - 2(2) = -12 \Rightarrow x_2 = 1$
  • $2x_1 + 1 + 2 = 5 \Rightarrow x_1 = 1$

Solution: $x=(1,1,2)$. Check it in the original equations: $2+1+2=5$ ✓, $4-6=-2$ ✓, $-2+7+4=9$ ✓.

Preview of $A=LU$. Store the multipliers in a lower triangular matrix with 1s on the diagonal: $$ L=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\2&1&0\-1&-1&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad LU = A \ \text{(verified)}. $$ Elimination is the factorization $A=LU$. If a zero shows up in a pivot position, you must swap rows. A permutation matrix $P$ records the swaps, which gives $PA=LU$.

Preview of Gauss–Jordan. Keep going: also clear the entries above each pivot, and divide each row by its pivot. This leads to the reduced row echelon form (RREF). If you run it on $[A\mid I]$, you end with $[I\mid A^{-1}]$. That is how you compute an inverse by hand.

Cost. Elimination on an $n\times n$ matrix takes about $\tfrac{1}{3}n^3$ multiplications. This motivates iterative methods for very large systems.

➜ Where you’ll use this: first block of your syllabus (Gaussian elimination, $PA=LU$, Gauss–Jordan). Pivots also give the rank (Section 8) and the determinant (Section 9).

7. The inverse matrix

A square matrix $A$ is invertible if there is a matrix $A^{-1}$ with $AA^{-1}=A^{-1}A=I$. Then $Ax=b$ has the unique solution $x=A^{-1}b$. (In practice you solve by elimination. You almost never compute $A^{-1}$ explicitly.)

The 2×2 formula (memorise it): $$ \begin{bmatrix}a&b\c&d\end{bmatrix}^{-1}=\frac{1}{ad-bc}\begin{bmatrix}d&-b\-c&a\end{bmatrix},\qquad ad-bc\neq0. $$ Example: $\begin{bmatrix}2&1\1&1\end{bmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\-1&2\end{bmatrix}$ (here $ad-bc=1$).

Rules: $(AB)^{-1}=B^{-1}A^{-1}$; $(A^T)^{-1}=(A^{-1})^T$; the inverse of a diagonal matrix inverts each diagonal entry.

The “Invertible Matrix Theorem” (all equivalent for a square $n\times n$ matrix $A$):

  • $A$ is invertible
  • $Ax=b$ has exactly one solution for every $b$
  • $Ax=0$ has only the solution $x=0$
  • elimination produces $n$ nonzero pivots
  • the columns (and rows) of $A$ are linearly independent
  • $\operatorname{rank}A = n$
  • $\det A \neq 0$
  • $0$ is not an eigenvalue of $A$

This one list connects almost every topic in your course. Revisit it often.

8. Span, independence, basis, rank

  • Span of $v_1,\dots,v_k$: the set of all their linear combinations.
  • Linearly independent: the only combination $c_1v_1+\dots+c_kv_k$ that gives $0$ is the one with all $c_i=0$. Informally, no vector is redundant.
  • Basis of a space: an independent set that spans it.
  • Dimension: the number of vectors in any basis.
  • Subspace: a set of vectors closed under addition and scalar multiplication (it must contain $0$). Examples are lines and planes through the origin.
  • Rank of $A$: the number of pivots = the number of independent columns = the number of independent rows.

Example. $\begin{bmatrix}1&2\2&4\end{bmatrix}$ has rank 1, because column 2 = 2 × column 1.

The four fundamental subspaces of an $m\times n$ matrix $A$ of rank $r$ (your course proves this; here is just the map):

Subspace Lives in Dimension
Column space $C(A)$ $\mathbb{R}^m$ $r$
Null space $N(A)$ (solutions of $Ax=0$) $\mathbb{R}^n$ $n-r$
Row space $C(A^T)$ $\mathbb{R}^n$ $r$
Left null space $N(A^T)$ $\mathbb{R}^m$ $m-r$

Row space ⟂ null space, and column space ⟂ left null space. Together, the dimensions and the orthogonality form the “fundamental theorem of linear algebra” in Strang’s sense.

9. Determinants

The determinant is a single number attached to a square matrix.

2×2: $\det\begin{bmatrix}a&b\c&d\end{bmatrix}=ad-bc$.

3×3 (cofactor expansion along row 1): $$ \det\begin{bmatrix}a&b&c\d&e&f\g&h&i\end{bmatrix}=a(ei-fh)-b(di-fg)+c(dh-eg). $$ Example: $\det\begin{bmatrix}1&2&3\0&4&5\1&0&6\end{bmatrix}=1(24)-2(-5)+3(-4)=22$.

Geometric meaning. $|\det A|$ is the factor by which $A$ scales area (in 2D) or volume (in 3D). The sign tells you whether orientation is flipped. $\det A=0$ means $A$ flattens space into a lower dimension, so $A$ is not invertible.

Key properties:

  • $\det(AB)=\det A\cdot\det B$
  • $\det A^T=\det A$
  • $\det A^{-1}=1/\det A$
  • For a triangular matrix, $\det$ = product of the diagonal entries
  • A row swap flips the sign; subtracting a multiple of one row from another changes nothing
  • $\det(cA)=c^n\det A$ for $n\times n$ (a common trap)

Fast method: $\det A = \pm$(product of pivots). For the example in Section 6, $\det A = 2\cdot(-8)\cdot 1=-16$ (no row swaps, so the sign is +).

10. Eigenvalues and eigenvectors (preview)

A nonzero vector $v$ is an eigenvector of a square matrix $A$ if $A$ only stretches it: $$ Av=\lambda v . $$ The scalar $\lambda$ is the eigenvalue. The direction of $v$ is unchanged (or reversed if $\lambda<0$).

How to find them. $Av=\lambda v \iff (A-\lambda I)v=0$ has a nonzero solution $\iff \det(A-\lambda I)=0$. This is the characteristic polynomial.

Worked example. $A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\1&2\end{bmatrix}$. $$ \det\begin{bmatrix}2-\lambda&1\1&2-\lambda\end{bmatrix}=(2-\lambda)^2-1=\lambda^2-4\lambda+3=(\lambda-1)(\lambda-3). $$

  • $\lambda_1=3$: solve $(A-3I)v=0$, which gives $v_1=(1,1)$
  • $\lambda_2=1$: solve $(A-I)v=0$, which gives $v_2=(1,-1)$

Check: $A(1,1)=(3,3)=3(1,1)$ ✓.

Notice that $A$ is symmetric and its eigenvectors are orthogonal. That is the spectral theorem in miniature: $A=Q\Lambda Q^T$.

Two handy checks: $\lambda_1+\dots+\lambda_n=\operatorname{tr}A$ and $\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_n=\det A$. Here $3+1=4=\operatorname{tr}A$ and $3\cdot1=3=\det A$ ✓.

Triangular matrices: the eigenvalues are the diagonal entries. Example: $\begin{bmatrix}3&1\0&2\end{bmatrix}$ has $\lambda=3,2$.

Why it matters. If $A=V\Lambda V^{-1}$ (diagonalization), then $A^k=V\Lambda^kV^{-1}$. Powers, and functions such as $e^{At}$, become easy. That is the basis of “functions of matrices” and of linear dynamical systems $x_{k+1}=Ax_k$ or $\dot{x}=Ax$.

Quadratic forms (preview). For symmetric $A$, the scalar $x^TAx$ is a quadratic form: $$ x^T\begin{bmatrix}2&1\1&2\end{bmatrix}x = 2x_1^2+2x_1x_2+2x_2^2 . $$ $A$ is positive definite if $x^TAx>0$ for all $x\neq0$. This happens exactly when all its eigenvalues are positive.

11. Orthogonality and projections

Orthonormal vectors: mutually orthogonal, each of length 1. Put them as columns of $Q$; then $Q^TQ=I$. If $Q$ is square, $Q^{-1}=Q^T$. Orthogonal matrices preserve lengths: $\lVert Qx\rVert=\lVert x\rVert$.

Projection onto a line through $a$: the closest point to $b$ on that line is $$ p=\frac{a^Tb}{a^Ta},a, \qquad e=b-p\ \perp\ a . $$ Example: $a=(1,1)$, $b=(2,0)$ gives $p=\tfrac{2}{2}(1,1)=(1,1)$ and $e=(1,-1)$. Check $e^Ta=0$ ✓.

Projection onto the column space of $A$ (independent columns): $$ p = A\hat{x},\quad \text{where } A^TA\hat{x}=A^Tb . $$ These are the normal equations. The error $e=b-A\hat{x}$ is orthogonal to every column of $A$, i.e. $A^Te=0$.

Gram–Schmidt turns independent columns into orthonormal ones. Recording the process gives $A=QR$ ($R$ upper triangular).

12. Norms and the condition number (preview)

Vector norms:

  • $\lVert x\rVert_2=\sqrt{\sum x_i^2}$ (Euclidean)
  • $\lVert x\rVert_1=\sum|x_i|$
  • $\lVert x\rVert_\infty=\max|x_i|$

Matrix norm (induced): $\lVert A\rVert=\max_{x\neq0}\dfrac{\lVert Ax\rVert}{\lVert x\rVert}$, the largest stretching factor. In the 2-norm it equals the largest singular value $\sigma_{\max}$.

Condition number: $\kappa(A)=\lVert A\rVert,\lVert A^{-1}\rVert$ (in the 2-norm, $\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$). It measures how much relative errors in $b$ can be amplified in $x$. Example: $\begin{bmatrix}1&1\1&1.0001\end{bmatrix}$ is almost singular, and its $\kappa\approx 4\times10^4$. A tiny change in $b$ can change $x$ enormously. Tikhonov regularization, which solves $(A^TA+\alpha I)x=A^Tb$, is one remedy for such ill-conditioned problems.

13. A little matrix calculus (for least squares)

For a scalar function $f(x)$ of a vector $x\in\mathbb{R}^n$, the gradient is the vector of partial derivatives $\nabla f=\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1},\dots,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}\right)^T$. A minimum requires $\nabla f = 0$.

The only three rules you need:

$f(x)$ $\nabla f(x)$ Analogy in 1D
$a^Tx$ $a$ $(ax)’ = a$
$x^TAx$ $(A+A^T)x$, which is $2Ax$ if $A$ is symmetric $(ax^2)’=2ax$
$\lVert Ax-b\rVert^2$ $2A^T(Ax-b)$ $((ax-b)^2)’ = 2a(ax-b)$

Derivation of the normal equations. Expand: $$ f(x)=\lVert Ax-b\rVert^2=(Ax-b)^T(Ax-b)=x^TA^TAx-2b^TAx+b^Tb . $$ Using the rules (note that $A^TA$ is symmetric): $$ \nabla f = 2A^TAx-2A^Tb=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{A^TA\hat{x}=A^Tb}. $$ You get the same equations as from the geometric projection in Section 11. Calculus and geometry agree.

Worked example (fitting a line). Fit $y=c+dt$ to the points $(t,y)=(0,6),(1,0),(2,0)$: $$ A=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\1&1\1&2\end{bmatrix},\ b=\begin{bmatrix}6\0\0\end{bmatrix},\quad A^TA=\begin{bmatrix}3&3\3&5\end{bmatrix},\ A^Tb=\begin{bmatrix}6\0\end{bmatrix} \ \Rightarrow\ \hat{x}=\begin{bmatrix}c\d\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}5\-3\end{bmatrix}. $$ The best line is $y=5-3t$.

Tikhonov version: minimise $\lVert Ax-b\rVert^2+\alpha\lVert x\rVert^2$. The gradient is $2A^T(Ax-b)+2\alpha x = 0$, which gives $(A^TA+\alpha I)x=A^Tb$.

For a large table of identities, see The Matrix Cookbook (References).

15. Exercises with answers

Try each one before reading the answer.

  1. Compute $\begin{bmatrix}1&0&2\-1&3&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\2\3\end{bmatrix}$ both by rows and by columns.
  2. Let $A$ be $3\times 4$ and $B$ be $4\times 2$. What is the size of $AB$? Is $BA$ defined?
  3. Find $A^T$ and $A^TA$ for $A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\0&1\1&0\end{bmatrix}$. Is $A^TA$ symmetric?
  4. Solve by elimination: $x+2y=5$, $3x+4y=6$.
  5. Invert $\begin{bmatrix}3&1\5&2\end{bmatrix}$.
  6. Are $(1,2,3)$, $(2,4,6)$, $(0,1,0)$ linearly independent?
  7. Find the eigenvalues of $\begin{bmatrix}4&1\2&3\end{bmatrix}$.
  8. Project $b=(3,1)$ onto the line through $a=(1,0)$.
  9. Compute $\det\begin{bmatrix}2&0&0\5&3&0\1&7&4\end{bmatrix}$.
  10. Write $3x_1^2 + 4x_1x_2 + x_2^2$ as $x^TAx$ with $A$ symmetric.
Answers
  1. Rows: $(1+0+6,,-1+6+3)=(7,8)$. Columns: $1(1,-1)+2(0,3)+3(2,1)=(7,8)$.
  2. $AB$ is $3\times2$. $BA$ would be $(4\times2)(3\times4)$: not defined, because 2 ≠ 3.
  3. $A^T=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&1\2&1&0\end{bmatrix}$, $A^TA=\begin{bmatrix}2&2\2&5\end{bmatrix}$. Yes, $A^TA$ is always symmetric, since $(A^TA)^T=A^TA$.
  4. Row 2 − 3·Row 1 gives $-2y=-9$, so $y=4.5$ and $x=5-9=-4$.
  5. $\det=6-5=1$, so the inverse is $\begin{bmatrix}2&-1\-5&3\end{bmatrix}$.
  6. No. $(2,4,6)=2(1,2,3)$.
  7. $\lambda^2-7\lambda+10=0$, so $\lambda=5,2$. Check: sum 7 = trace, product 10 = det.
  8. $p=\tfrac{3}{1}(1,0)=(3,0)$ and $e=(0,1)\perp a$.
  9. The matrix is triangular, so $\det = 2\cdot3\cdot4=24$.
  10. $A=\begin{bmatrix}3&2\2&1\end{bmatrix}$. The off-diagonal cross term $4x_1x_2$ is split in half between the two positions.
