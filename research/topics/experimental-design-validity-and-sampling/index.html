Summary

A course-level vocabulary for judging whether an experiment can support a causal claim (internal validity), whether the claim carries to other people and settings (external validity), and whether the sample and the measures are adequate. The note is a toolbox for designing our own games and for auditing the studies in the library. It adds no domain evidence on commons games. Its link to External Validity of Experimental Games is that that note covers how well game behaviour predicts behaviour outside the game, and this note covers the design and sampling choices that sit under that question.

Claims

  1. What counts as scientific. Claims should be empirically testable, replicable, objective, transparent, falsifiable and logically consistent. The notes name the hypothetico-deductive method with replication as the current standard. A confirmation is never conclusive, and hypotheses are provisionally supported, not proven. Duhem–Quine is noted as the point that no hypothesis can be verified in isolation, and Hume’s problem of induction as the point that new observations never conclusively show truth (Zand Scholten (Coursera), pages 1, 2, 4).
  2. Four conditions for a causal claim. Cause and effect are connected, the cause precedes the effect, they covary consistently, and no alternative explanation remains. The last condition is marked “difficult”. Correlation does not imply causation (Zand Scholten (Coursera), page 4).
  3. Types of validity and reliability. Construct validity: measured or manipulated as intended. Internal validity: the causal relation holds and alternative explanations are implausible. External validity: results generalise to other settings. Reliability means consistency, stability and precision, and is required for validity but not the reverse. Systematic error threatens validity, random error threatens reliability (Zand Scholten (Coursera), pages 4, 10).
  4. Threats to internal validity, grouped by source. Participants: maturation (remedy: control group), selection and selection-by-maturation (remedy: randomisation). Instruments: low construct validity, instrumentation, testing or sensitisation. Artificiality: experimenter expectancy (remedy: experimenter-blind design) and demand characteristics (remedy: double-blind design and a cover story). Setup: ambiguous temporal precedence, history (an event that offers an alternative cause) and mortality (drop-outs, a problem when they differ between conditions; remedy: document the reasons) (Zand Scholten (Coursera), pages 4, 5).
  5. Kinds of extraneous variables. A confounder (lurking variable) relates to both independent and dependent variable and is usually not measured. A control variable is the same but is part of the study and measured. Background variables (age, sex, education and similar) bear on generalisability and are controlled or held constant (Zand Scholten (Coursera), page 5).
  6. True experiment. It rests on manipulation (the cause is under full control and precedes the effect), comparison (a condition where the cause is absent), random assignment and replication. Factorial designs vary several factors at once and yield main effects and interaction effects. Between-subject designs split participants over conditions and within-subject designs expose everyone to all conditions. In within designs the order of conditions must be randomised and counterbalanced, with blocking so that no order is over- or under-represented, otherwise maturation or habituation can bias the result (Zand Scholten (Coursera), pages 6, 7).
  7. Randomisation can fail with small groups. The notes recommend a randomisation check and restricted randomisation (blocking for equal group size, or for several characteristics combined). A manipulation check should come after the dependent variable is measured, so that it does not change behaviour (Zand Scholten (Coursera), page 7).
  8. Standard experimental designs. Two-group, two-group pre/post (useful against maturation and to quantify change, but a pretest can sensitise participants or reveal the purpose), Solomon four-group and within-subject repeated measures (Zand Scholten (Coursera), page 7).
  9. Quasi-experimental and correlational designs. When random assignment is impossible (pragmatic or ethical reasons, or the independent variable is an individual difference), the study is quasi-experimental and tries to approximate randomisation by matching. Matching suffers from undermatching caused by regression towards the mean. Standard quasi-experimental designs: static group comparison, pre/post with a non-equivalent control group, interrupted time series (exposed to history threats) and replicated interrupted time series with a control group. Correlational designs only measure: cross-sectional, time series, and panel or time-series cross-sectional (TSCS) designs, with cohorts as a variant (Zand Scholten (Coursera), pages 8, 9).
  10. Laboratory versus field. Lab settings maximise internal validity at the cost of ecological validity; field settings are closer to the natural occurrence of the phenomenon. The ideal is ceteris paribus (conditions identical except for the independent variable), which is very hard to reach in the social sciences (Zand Scholten (Coursera), page 7).
  11. Threats to external validity. History threat (other times), setting threat (other environments), pretesting and reactivity (expectations, nervousness), and selection threat (problems with the sample). Remedies for selection: random sampling and replication (Zand Scholten (Coursera), page 12).
  12. Probability sampling needs a sampling frame. Types: simple random, systematic, stratified random (weighted by stratum size) and multi-stage cluster sampling. The last two reduce cost and effort but give a larger margin of error (Zand Scholten (Coursera), page 13).
  13. Non-probability sampling. Quota, purposive, convenience (high bias, no frame needed) and snowball sampling. A margin of error is not available and representativeness is not claimed, so generalisations need caution (Zand Scholten (Coursera), page 14).
  14. Sampling error and non-sampling error. Sampling error comes from randomness and can be estimated through the standard error, margin of error and confidence interval. Non-sampling error covers sampling bias (systematic under-representation), non-response, an inaccurate frame, data collection, data entry and data processing. It is hard to assess and can be random or systematic (Zand Scholten (Coursera), pages 14, 15).
  15. Sample size. Bigger is better, but the gain in margin of error shrinks as the sample grows (not linear). A more heterogeneous population needs a larger sample. The minimum sample size follows from the desired margin of error, the confidence level and an estimate of the variability, plus a compensation for non-response (Zand Scholten (Coursera), page 16).
  16. Measurement. Levels: nominal, ordinal, interval (arbitrary zero), ratio (non-arbitrary zero). Variables can be categorised on purpose, losing interval and ratio information. Validity types for instruments: face, predictive or criterion, convergent and discriminant (multi-trait multi-method matrix). Reliability types: test-retest, internal consistency, intra-observer and inter-rater. Self-report is exposed to response styles: acquiescence, social desirability, extreme responding and bias towards the middle (Zand Scholten (Coursera), pages 9 to 11).

Inferences

  1. Our games are experiments only if condition assignment is randomised at a stated level. If conditions are introduced over time inside persistent timelines, the course’s within-subject logic applies: order and timing must be randomised or counterbalanced with blocking (claim 6), and a randomisation check is needed because the number of independent units is small (claim 7).
  2. Long-running play invites several named threats. Repeated sessions bring testing and sensitisation, calendar-time history events and maturation of the player pool (claims 4, 6). Drop-outs are mortality, and their reasons should be logged, especially if they differ by condition.
  3. A manipulation check belongs after the outcome is measured. For an information treatment (for example threshold transparency), a post-game question on what players perceived serves this role. It matches the post-game module proposed in External Validity of Experimental Games (inference 4).
  4. A cover story and demand characteristics are one problem. Players who know the condition may act on what they think the experimenter wants (claim 4). The framing of a commons game as a commons game can invite this, so the wording of the instructions is a design choice to record.
  5. An open call yields a convenience or snowball sample. Under claims 12 to 14 the study can support statements about the effect of our conditions on these participants (internal validity), not about population levels. Directional claims are the safer target, as in External Validity of Experimental Games (inference 3).
  6. The review checklist can be applied to the library. The course assignment (page 16) asks for a critique of the manipulation, the measurement instruments, external validity and the sampling method. The same four headings can screen the empirical sources before a claim is carried into an argument note.
  7. Measurement level decides the analysis. Ratio outcomes (for example units extracted) and ordinal ones (Likert items) call for different summaries (claim 16). Each outcome in a design should carry its level.

Limits

  • The only source is a student’s handwritten notes from one course, transcribed from scans. No primary literature is cited, and the notes are secondary and compressed.
  • One statement looks like a slip: scan page 16 says the margin of error depends on the relative size of the sample to the population, not on its absolute size. Standard teaching says the reverse for large populations (absolute sample size matters). Claim 15 therefore leaves it out. Verify against a textbook before use.
  • The history-of-science timeline on pages 2 and 3 is a list of names and dates and is not used here.
  • The course follows psychology and social-science conventions. It treats deception as sometimes necessary with checks (page 17). Experimental economics usually avoids deception, and the notes do not cover incentivised design.
  • The classification of time-series designs (correlational, quasi-experimental or experimental depending on the unit) is the teacher’s and may not match other textbooks.
  • Page 16 includes an assignment critique of a violent-video-game study. It is practice material and is not used as evidence.

Open

  • Does a persistent-timeline design count as a true experiment, a quasi-experiment or a panel design, and what randomisation can honestly be claimed?
  • Which of the four unit levels (round, tenure, timeline, lineage) is the unit of assignment?
  • How should the manipulation check be worded so that it does not leak the hypothesis to later players?
  • What do the experimental-economics rules on deception and cover stories allow at our host institutions?
